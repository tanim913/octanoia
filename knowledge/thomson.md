## PART A: Thomson's structural model, and what it means for scoring

### A1. The left-brain/right-brain "compass"

Jung called the four functions "compass points for the personality, by which we are oriented to our immediate situation" (p.6). Thomson turns this into a 2x2 brain grid (p.69). Every function chapter opens with that type's version of it (pp.145, 169, 196, 222, 253, 286, 317, 365).

|  | LEFT hemisphere (verbal, sequential, one-at-a-time) | RIGHT hemisphere (imagistic, all-at-once, pattern) |
|---|---|---|
| **Front (Extraverted)** | Te, Fe | Ne, Se |
| **Back (Introverted)** | Si, Ni | Fi, Ti |

The rules she draws from the grid:
- **Same function, opposite attitude means opposite hemisphere.** "Introverted and Extraverted versions of the same function activate on opposite sides of the brain" (p.73). Te and Fe are left-brain, but Ti and Fi are right-brain. Se and Ne are right-brain, but Si and Ni are left-brain.
- **The dominant and secondary always sit in the same hemisphere.** "Our dominant and secondary functions are compatible because their primary activity takes place in the same brain hemisphere. Indeed, we may resist our less-developed functions, in part, because they require us to process information in an unaccustomed left- or right-brain way" (p.73).
- **J types are left-brain types and P types are right-brain types** (pp.71–72). Every J type pairs extraverted judgment with introverted perception (Te/Fe with Si/Ni, all left). Every P type pairs extraverted perception with introverted judgment (Se/Ne with Ti/Fi, all right).
- **She gives a left/right trait table** (pp.71–72):
  - Left: event-oriented, general/abstract, words and numbers, seeks an exact conclusion, step by step, temporal.
  - Right: idea-oriented, concrete/specific, patterns and pictures, content with approximate and evolving solutions, perceives all at once, unaware of time limits, evaluates intonation.
  - Learning style: "TJs may need to hear something verbalized or see it written … FJs may need behavioral signs … SPs … prefer illustrations or examples … NPs respond better to metaphor" (pp.72–73).
- **In each chapter-opening diagram:**
  - The dominant and secondary occupy one hemisphere column, one Extraverted and one Introverted.
  - The tertiary and inferior occupy the other column.
  - The tertiary shares the dominant's attitude; the inferior shares the secondary's attitude.
  - Example (Ni chapter, p.222): left column holds Secondary Je (top) and Dominant Ni (bottom). Right column holds Inferior Se (top) and Tertiary Ji (bottom).

**Caveat for a "transparent" site.** Thomson presents this as PET-scan research. Her endnote (p.405) credits the diagram to Jonathan Niednagel, *How to Choose Your Best Sport and Play It* (1992). Niednagel's "brain typing" is not accepted neuroscience. Use the grid as a metaphor or visual, not as a scientific claim. She herself calls type theory "a description of—not a prescription for—human behavior … useful in the way that wisdom stories are useful" (p.11).

### A2. The axes ("direct opposites")

Thomson never uses the word "axis." She speaks of each function's "direct opposite," and her model contains exactly four opposite pairs:
- **Te / Fi** ("its direct opposite, Extraverted Thinking," p.367)
- **Fe / Ti** ("Introverted Thinking (Feeling's direct opposite)," p.77)
- **Se / Ni**
- **Si / Ne** ("Extraverted Intuition … its opposite, Introverted Sensation," p.218)

Each pair differs in both function and attitude, so the partners sit diagonally across the grid in opposite hemispheres. Two relationships hold for every type:
- **Dominant and inferior** are direct opposites (for example, Te-dominant has Fi inferior).
- **Secondary and tertiary** are direct opposites (for ESTJ, secondary Si has tertiary Ne).

Opposites exclude each other in the moment. Sensation and Intuition "can't be used at the same time" (p.34). "Whenever we employ a function or attitude, we are axiomatically choosing against its opposite" (p.25).

### A3. Her eight-slot "type lasagna" and the "ship's roster"

Thomson's full stack has eight named roles, not four (pp.78–79, 88–91, and the box on every type profile). Take the dominant as function X in attitude a, and the secondary as function Y in attitude b. X' means "the other function in X's pair" (T and F swap, S and N swap).

| Slot | Formula | Example: ESTJ | Example: INTJ | Role in her "ship" metaphor (pp.87–91) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Dominant | X-a | Te | Ni | Captain; "subject to our will … feel[s] like 'us'" |
| 2. Secondary | Y-b | Si | Te | Petty officer; follows orders and offers alternate views; "less conscious," needs "deliberate effort" |
| 3–4. **Alternatives** (same hemisphere) | X'-a, Y'-b | Fe, Ni | Si, Fe | **Crow's nest**: "the first functions we turn to for solutions"; they compete with the preferred function but, being in the same hemisphere, are "used … fairly well — when [we] recognized their logical utility" (p.89) |
| 5–6. **Double agents** (opposite hemisphere) | Y-a, X-b | Se, Ti | Ti, Ne | **Maintenance crew**: give "needed balance when things [are] going smoothly," but "tend to mutiny" under stress, pulling us toward inferior attitudes (p.90) |
| 7. Tertiary | Y'-a | Ne | Fi | **Water-skier** "shouting rude remarks"; opportunistic; "coasting on our dominant momentum" |
| 8. Inferior | X'-b | Fi | Se | **Would-be captain** put off the ship in a lifeboat that stays tied to the hull by a rope; it eventually drags the ship to a standstill |

**Key point for scoring.** In Thomson's model the "tertiary" is not the third-strongest function. "At the very bottom are the two functions directly opposed to the top two: the tertiary and inferior functions. These are our least-conscious functions" (p.78). The four middle functions sit between them and "support our specialized aims … They don't exert much influence on our choices as such, unless we make a deliberate effort" (p.86). Her tertiary takes the dominant's attitude (INTJ = Ni-Te-Fi-Se; ENFP = Ne-Fi-Te-Si), matching the common modern four-letter stack.

### A4. What the model implies for a scoring engine

These are my inferences, each tied to the text that supports it.

1. **Opposite pairs should correlate negatively on approach and trust, not on skill.** Thomson separates being able to use a function's skills from living by its approach to life:
   - "ESPs have no problem using Introverted Intuitive skills when they need them. It's the Introverted Intuitive approach to life that plays havoc with a Sensate point of view" (p.153).
   - "All types are capable of using the skills their inferior function offers … What's at issue here is the approach to life [it] makes possible" (p.343; same point p.384).
   - INJs, whose inferior is Se, still "follow sports, … drive fast cars, cook gourmet meals"; "Physicality, for these types, is quite nearly another conceptual viewpoint" (p.232).
   - Design implication: write items that ask what you default to, trust, or experience as "me." Avoid "can you do X" items, which will blur the axes.
2. **The inferior is present, just unconscious and disowned.** "Our inferior function isn't merely undeveloped. Its point of view is genuinely inferior. The approach to life it fosters has no relationship to our conscious position" (p.82). It is "archaic and primitive" (p.83). It shows up as projection and stress (the "alien hand," pp.76–77; the "untended yard," p.114). So a dominant-Ni person should score low on Se as a preferred approach, but may endorse Se-flavored stress items (compulsive sensory fixation, anxious possessiveness, a sense of being watched).
   - Recommendation: keep "stress-grip" items in a separate scale. Read them as confirming the opposite dominant, and do not add them to the function's preference score.
3. **Same-hemisphere functions should co-vary positively.** The dominant and secondary always share a hemisphere, and the alternatives sit there too. A plausible ESTJ profile would be:
   - Te and Si high
   - Fe and Ni moderate (crow's nest)
   - Se and Ti moderate to low (double agents)
   - Ne low and Fi lowest
   - More generally, J types should lean toward the left cluster (Te, Fe, Si, Ni) and P types toward the right cluster (Se, Ne, Ti, Fi). Hemisphere is a real latent dimension in her model, and it equals J/P.
4. **Compute a type-fit score.** For each of the 16 stacks, compare the user's 8-function vector to the template ordering: dominant > secondary > alternatives ≥ double agents > tertiary > inferior. Report the best fit and the runner-up. Her own text supports surfacing close calls (A5).
5. **The dominant/secondary balance depends on attitude.**
   - Extraverts "can ignore their Introverted side for half a lifetime" (p.64), so the secondary may score low in extraverts, especially young ones.
   - Introverts "have little choice" and must develop the extraverted secondary (p.64), but it can be a defensive persona.
   - "a very high P or J score (12 to 14), for an Introverted type, doesn't usually indicate balanced Extraverted development. It suggests an attempt to keep the inner self intact by meeting others' surface expectations" (p.65; also pp.66, 68).
   - So an introvert's high extraverted-secondary score may reflect a mask. Consider items that separate "what I do at work or in public" from "what I'd do left alone."
6. **Public versus private context matters.** "P and J traits are most pronounced in our dealings with the outer world. When we're alone … we may exhibit some traits that conflict with our P or J designation … TJs may be highly task-oriented in their career objectives, but somewhat indecisive in their personal lives. FPs may be highly flexible in social situations, but exacting in areas important to themselves" (p.48). Extreme introverts' self-experience "coincides strongly with many traits opposed to their P or J designation" (p.48). Tag items by context.
7. **Expect development and stress patterns:**
   - Younger respondents: the dominant is exaggerated and the secondary underused.
   - Midlife: "close scores in every category" and pressure from the inferior (pp.26, 32, 76).
   - Defensive tertiary use can make the tertiary look strong for a while (A6).
   - Results pages can explain a "flat" or "odd" profile through these patterns.
8. **Development advice: work on the secondary, not the inferior** (pp.80–85, Maggie's story). Results text should send people to the secondary for growth.

### A5. Thomson's caveats against boxing people (quotable)

- "Type theory, in this respect, is a description of—not a prescription for—human behavior. It's useful in the way that wisdom stories are useful." (p.11)
- "Our type does not define who we 'really are' any more than using a particular language defines the nature of the soul." (p.23) Her native-language metaphor: all babies babble every sound, and we keep an "accent" in our less-developed functions (pp.23–24).
- "These generalizations, however, should not be construed as stereotypes. Psychological type is not an internal tape loop that irrevocably determines people's options … Type is only a description of how we've learned to communicate. What we communicate is another matter entirely." (p.24)
- "It's only the overdevelopment of one function to the exclusion of others that constricts a personality." (p.24)
- "In the realm of nature, perfect balance is not a good thing … close or even scores in a category (8/6 or 7/7) tend to suggest that the person hasn't developed a clear-cut sense of self. This is particularly true for people under thirty." (p.25)
- "A type test can't get at the content of one's inner life. Close scores merely indicate the absence of a developed exterior form." (p.26)
- "No one can be entirely Introverted or Extraverted." (p.30) "All types use both attitudes." (p.124)
- "Good type development, in this respect, doesn't mean using all our functions equally well. It means establishing a psychological structure strong enough to contain and unify them in a shared enterprise." (p.97)
- "No particular combination of functions is inherently better or preferable to another." (p.8)
- "Typological imbalance isn't a sign that something has gone wrong. It's the natural result of the human path of development." (p.76)
- Whole-elephant parable: "The people we understand least usually represent the parts of our psychological heritage that we've left behind." (pp.11–12)

**Development over the life course:**
- Puberty channels the strongest function into a social identity (p.76).
- From adolescence to early midlife it is normal to refine the dominant and use the secondary only in its support (p.56).
- "Many Extraverts don't even acknowledge their Introverted side until midlife … Some Introverts spend the first half of life trying to make a place for themselves … [and] feel that they've just 'found themselves' at midlife" (p.32).
- "We bargain the undifferentiated wholeness of childhood for the power to choose, then recover it again, as a unified perspective on life" (p.97).
- Feeling "stuck" is the classic sign of a "crisis of the secondary function" (p.84).

### A6. The tertiary and inferior in her dynamics

- **What the tertiary does in a crisis:** it "will tell us exactly what we want to hear: that the conflict we're experiencing is not our fault" (p.98). "The tertiary function is dangerous in a crisis because the point of view it encourages is not so much wrong as it is misplaced" (p.94). "Once we're persuaded that our inner conflicts are outside enemies, our only options are fight or flight. And the tertiary function always counsels flight" (p.95). It "offers platitudes—ideas that are true in general but have no productive relationship to the situation" (p.103).
- **The tertiary when healthy:** "Used in conjunction with our best two, the tertiary function … grounds the aims of our stronger functions by introducing us to our genuine limitations—of time, ability, and opportunity" (pp.112–113).
- **Inferior introversion in extraverts:** "self-centered and defensive." The person feels "they're not getting what they really need" (p.99).
- **Inferior extraversion in introverts:** "other-oriented and aggressive … the will to power and acquisition, the desire to be seen and admired and wanted"; they "call attention to themselves" (p.106).
- **Inferiors flare in relationships with opposite types.** When a Thinker and a Feeler argue, each slips into a caricature of the other: "You don't appreciate me! … you want a damned mind reader!" versus "Whenever I ask for support … you always make me feel stupid!" (p.115).
- **The fix is to shift to a non-competing function.** Kirk and Scotty meet through Ti; Tuvok is reached through Si practicality (pp.116–119). The Golden Rule fails across types (p.114).

**Her extended case studies** (good storytelling templates for results pages):
- **Maggie, INTJ** (pp.80–85, 107–110). She tries to "develop" inferior Se by competing for train seats with a briefcase and becomes a "Mad Max" figure. Later, an inferior-Se/tertiary-Fi spiral sets in: obsessive dream journals, the "right tablets," unpaid bills. She recovers through secondary Te: modeling the A+ papers to make a skeptical professor understand.
- **Grant, ESTJ** (pp.88–95). Under inferior Fi he becomes a righteous "lone fighter for justice" and gets fired. Under tertiary Ne he daydreams on used-car lots about pickup trucks and heading west. He needed secondary Si: what actually matters to him.
- **Jake, ENFP** (pp.100–105). A charming improviser who cannot keep promises. Inferior Si makes him expect others to supply limits while resenting them. Tertiary Te makes others "unreasonable." He recovers through secondary Fi and finds a teaching job he values enough to show up on time.
- **Sharlene, ENFJ** (pp.110–112). She silently disagrees with the school board. Tertiary Se offers "just blurt it" as an escape hatch, which she rejects for the "high road." Secondary Ni lets her reframe her view toward shared goals.
- **Lorna and Michael, I/E couple** (pp.139–142). An "attitudinal symbiosis." Each partner carries the other's undeveloped attitude until both resent it; giving up expectations forces growth of the secondary.

### A7. How people misidentify themselves in self-report (her observations)

- **Social pressure flattens scores.** Extraverts in an introvert-majority seminar drifted to even E/I scores within a few sessions, and some EFJs tested as INFs. Extraverts "wanted me to give them an external reference point so they could establish their self-experience" (pp.127–128). The same happens when an introvert brings the test home to an extraverted spouse (p.128).
- **Gender socialization:** "Introverted men, in particular, often get scores that indicate slight Extraversion (8/6)" (p.33). Women are socialized toward Fe signs whatever their type (p.268). "Women's intuition" is mostly Sensation, humane traits, or socialization (pp.200–201).
- **Situational pressure:**
  - "ETPs … pushed … to soften an otherwise aggressive, impersonal approach … may get nearly even scores on T and F."
  - "ITJs who take a job that rewards good 'team' players may get close scores on P and J."
  - "An ENFP who marries another ENFP may assume more Sensate responsibilities," giving close S/N (p.25).
  - "FPs and FJs, in particular, need a certain degree of social support … Without it, they're reluctant to express their actual preferences, and most of their scores may be 8/6 or 7/7" (p.26).
  - An ESFJ "partnered romantically with a strong TJ … will get nearly even scores in all test categories" (p.352).
- **Life transitions** (new job, retirement, divorce) produce close E/I and P/J scores (p.33). After 35, "the ghosts of lost choices come back," giving near-even scores (p.26).
- **"Intuitive" is misapplied.** "Most of the people to whom we apply the word intuitive in this casual way aren't Intuitives … They're usually Sensates and Introverted P types, whose right-brain abilities the left brain can't explain to itself" (p.199). ESFPs "seem quite intuitive" but are "actually alert and observant to a very high degree" (p.164). Fi is "regard[ed] … as 'intuitive' or empathic" (p.365).
- **Ti doesn't feel like reasoning.** "This is why STPs appear to be (and believe themselves to be) using their reflexes when they're actually using their reason" (p.291). Ti "may not feel like 'being reasonable'" (p.288).
- **Fi doesn't feel like judging:** "Using Introverted Feeling doesn't make us 'feel' reasonable. We may not even feel like we're acting on 'knowledge'" (p.367).
- **Fe is not emotion.** Extreme Fe "leads to anything but a reliance on emotion" (p.321). ESFJs "may not know how they stand on an issue until they find out how others do" (pp.352–353).
- **Blind spots in self-image:**
  - ESJs "describe themselves as independent, even different from others" though well adjusted to standards. ISJs have a thoroughly individual view yet "rarely believe that their way of seeing things is unusual" (pp.178–179).
  - ISTJs "may experience themselves as undirected or indecisive" (p.188).
  - ISFJs see themselves as "too 'easy'" rather than controlling (pp.192–193).
  - ETJs "tend to believe they hardly ever get angry" (p.269).
  - INFJs "seem more outgoing than they really are" because of Fe (p.246).
  - ENTPs' "expansive nature and appetite for life can make them seem more Sensate than they really are" (p.213).
- **Introverts can look extraverted.** In congenial settings introverts "may seem quite Extraverted … strictly speaking, it isn't" Extraversion (p.134, Letterman; p.64). An INFP challenging people "'feels' like Extraverted behavior, but it isn't" (p.401). Defensive ISJs "can seem almost Extraverted" (p.184).
- **Identifying with an ideal inflates scores:** "people whose subjective experience has led them to champion this kind of vision almost always get high INFP scores" (p.381).
- **She disputes Keirsey's temperament lumping:**
  - SJ: ISJs and ESJs "don't really use their Judgment in the same way" (p.173).
  - ISTP vs. INTP: Keirsey sees ISTPs as just like ESTPs; Thomson argues the shared core is Ti, not surface behavior (pp.289–290).
  - ISFP vs. INFP: shared core Fi (p.372).
  - Lesson: a test must probe inner motive, not just outward behavior.
- **Answering instruction (her test, pp.13–14):** "Your answers should reflect how you actually see yourself, not how you would like to be … choose the one that you most agree with right now." If the result doesn't fit, "Don't underestimate a result of this kind! It can tell you about the way you're currently organizing your life."
- **Her test's item style** is useful as a model (pp.14–18). It uses forced A/B choices across contexts:
  - "If a heavy snowfall keeps you from going to school or work, do you A. wonder what you're missing? B. enjoy the unexpected time alone?"
  - Slogans: "People are apt to mistake the strength of their feeling for the strength of their argument" versus "Logic is the art of going wrong with confidence."

### 1. Extraverted Sensation (Se): right hemisphere, front. Ch 14, pp.145–168. Dominant in ESTP and ESFP; secondary in ISTP and ISFP; tertiary in ENTJ and ENFJ; inferior in INTJ and INFJ.

**Definition.** Se is acting in real time with the physical situation. Your actions change as the material in front of you changes, and the material changes in response. It is not just noticing things. Knowledge comes from doing something often enough to get a "feel" for it, and you gauge success by immediate visible effect. Socially, it is a feel for atmosphere, style, image, and the audience's reaction (pp.145–148).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Learns a dance, sport, instrument, or recipe by plunging in and repeating until it is felt. Instructions are dropped once the skill is "known" (pp.145–146).
2. In a crisis, responds immediately and practically to sensory cues rather than analyzing first (p.149).
3. Judges how a conversation or performance is going by the other person's visible reaction. With no reaction, teases or goads to get one, even "a disconcerted laugh" (p.149).
4. Relationships rest on doing things together and being physically present more than on shared ideas. Absence weakens the bond (pp.148–149).
5. Up to date on what is currently stylish or admired and may embody it. Aware of how things and people look (p.148; test item 13, p.15).
6. Loses interest quickly once a situation is "in hand" and moves to the next challenge (pp.149, 161).
7. Avoids or hands off problems that can't be solved by immediate action, or that involve things they don't understand (pp.149, 162, 165).
8. Seeks competition, risk, and intense sensation, and pushes physical limits. Without an outlet can become reckless (ESTP, p.160).
9. Prefers concrete facts with practical use. Impatient with abstract theory (p.161).
10. Becomes expert at variations of one familiar activity rather than starting entirely new ones (pp.162, 165–166).
11. Slow to accept a wholly novel idea; tests it against trusted people's experience first (pp.162, 166).
12. When overstimulated, copes by withdrawing or disappearing rather than setting limits in advance (pp.150, 167).

**What it is not**
- **Not mere awareness.** "Sensation … should not be confused with simple awareness … [it] is ultimately the ability to adapt to and make use of what we're taking in" (p.35).
- **Not Si, which is its "mirror image."** Se means "lose ourselves" in what is happening; Si means "find ourselves" in it, anchored by memory (p.170). The ESP becomes the epitome of popular taste; the ISJ tailors even a conventional job into self-expression (p.172). "The two forms of Sensation are not directly opposed" (p.170).
- **Not Ne.** Sensation attends to the object, Intuition to the context: "When summoned by a panther, don't anther" (p.197). Se people become the image others envy and imitate; Ne people are "screens for people's unarticulated hopes" (pp.202–203, 212).
- **Not Ti, though the two blend in ISTP and ESTP.** Ti is the tacit logic behind skilled action. ISTPs don't need novelty and won't fake interest; ESPs need novelty and "play along" (pp.307–308).
- **Not shallow.** "ESPs are generous and responsible, and they establish a strong code of honor" (p.152).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of INTJ and INFJ.** Maggie's train-seat aggression: hyper-alert, pumped, "replaying strategies … as if she'd been in a prizefight" (pp.81–82). Fixation on the concrete mechanics of a task (dream tablets), migraines, hyperawareness, neglected bills and groceries (pp.108–109). In romance INTJs feel "an anxious sense of material possession … impulsive, out of control," and press for declarations or permanency (p.244). Under its influence INJs show "undue interest in how they look to others," "a yearning for credit and recognition," and focus on surface criteria such as gender and race in language debates (pp.235–237).
- **As the tertiary of ENTJ and ENFJ.** ENTJs: "a strong attraction to graphic images of perceptual and visceral intensity"; acting on "primitive, unadapted Sensate impulses" as if that were authenticity (p.284). ENFJs: focus on others' pain while staying calm; urges to "liberate their pent-up emotions" or escape (pp.362–363). Sharlene's tertiary Se offered "say what you think, never mind approval" as an escape hatch (p.111).
- **When dominant Se is under-balanced.** ESTPs read their dissatisfaction as needing more stimulation and seek new challenges or audiences. ESFPs read it as depression and confusion and withdraw, using fatigue to escape stimulus (pp.162, 166–167).

**Stories and metaphors**
- Pirsig's mechanic: "The material and his thoughts are changing together in a progression of changes" (p.146).
- Kneading bread, moving a ball downcourt, playing in a band (p.146).
- The firefighter who loves heat on his skin and feels the same "heat" from an audience (p.147).
- The surfer on the perfect wave (pp.50, 148).
- Billy Crystal's Fernando: "It's better to look good than to feel good" (p.148).
- "Absence makes the heart grow fonder—of someone else" (pp.148–149).
- The ESFP who faked vegetarianism for the reactions it got (pp.151–152).
- Oskar Schindler, "a charming opportunist, a hustler," who used the same skills for a moral purpose (pp.157–158).
- Kwai Chang Caine and Due South's Benton Fraser, keen-sensed and compassionate (p.157).
- Peggy Sue Got Married versus Back to the Future (p.158).
- The ESFP bishop who insists on Mondays off for golf (p.60).
- Types: JFK, Madonna, Xena (ESTP); Princess Diana, Hercules (ESFP).

**Key quotes**
- "Every time our actions are changing immediately and directly in concert with our surface perceptions, we're drawing on Extraverted Sensation." (p.146)
- "More than any other type, ESPs believe that life is right now—explosive, impulsive, kinesthetic: a matter of doing, having, using things as they were meant to be used." (p.149)
- "Proactive change requires an Introverted viewpoint—the ability to stand back and measure a situation against one's inner needs and values." (p.151)

### 2. Introverted Sensation (Si): left hemisphere, back. Ch 15, pp.169–195. Dominant in ISTJ and ISFJ; secondary in ESTJ and ESFJ; tertiary in INTP and INFP; inferior in ENTP and ENFP.

**Definition.** Si selects the experiences that matter to you and stores them as facts, numbers, signs, and memories. It then steadies each new impression by fitting it to that store. The facts are highly selective and personal: they define your passions and become your self-experience. Present conditions have no stable meaning in themselves; your commitments and priorities give them meaning and continuity (pp.169–171). It is a left-brain, verbal, one-at-a-time function, and it is not rational. It gathers and keeps; it does not organize logically (pp.253–254).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Retains detailed facts in areas of personal investment and may forget facts outside them (World Series substitutions but not a friend's birthday) (p.170).
2. Collects objects or repeated experiences within one consistent, often offbeat domain: cylinder phonographs, roller coasters worldwide, every Richard Thompson concert within 500 miles, a Stephen King novel read ten times (pp.172, 179).
3. Reads contracts and documents word by word, sometimes several times. When pushed to decide early, keeps raising exceptions (p.178).
4. Needs to find a precedent or "box" for new information before accepting a change (the tribal elders) (pp.177–178).
5. Once convinced, is very hard to dissuade and wants hands-on control of carrying it out (p.178).
6. Keeps obligations even when circumstances change: "you meet your obligations" (beggar story, p.175).
7. Joins clubs and organizations in their field of interest and cares how they are run (p.172).
8. Keeps a private, ironic running commentary on gaps between expectation and reality (Frankie Valli filed under Vivaldi) (p.180).
9. Is exacting about certain routines (lunch at the same time) while indifferent to others (clutter on the floor) (pp.186–187).
10. Gets stuck on one detail of a story that doesn't add up before engaging with the rest (p.193).
11. Over-prepares for contingencies (three suitcases for a weekend) (p.194).
12. Is uncomfortable with misrepresentation of role or station (wrong clothes, displaying possessions beyond one's means) (p.180).
13. Becomes the person others rely on for tedious tasks and rarely refuses (p.181).

**What it is not**
- **Not conservatism or status-quo keeping.** "Far from supporting the status quo, ISJs have very firm ideas about 'how things should be'" (p.175). Extreme ISJs "can be diehard liberals" (p.176). ESJs swim with the tide of social standards; ISJs hold to inner priorities (p.174).
- **Not the "SJ" lump.** Thomson rejects grouping ISJs and ESJs together (p.173).
- **Not detached or dull.** "The inner world of an ISJ is often delightfully unconventional, even whimsical" (p.178). ISJs "are almost helplessly engaged" (p.180).
- **Not Se.** Si "packages" impressions and "takes them with us" (p.169).
- **Not Ni, its left-brain neighbor:**
  - The ISTJ accountant likes recording income but won't handle the money, keeping material reality under a stable mental framework. Ni does the opposite: "changing our frame of mind can change the world" (p.224).
  - ISJs preserve cherished objects all their lives; INJs collect emergent meaning and lose interest once it becomes public (pp.231–232).
  - ISTJs absorb new data by aligning it with what they know; INTJs by "rejecting its influence" (p.241).
- **Not rational judgment.** Si "offers no way to discriminate among [facts] rationally" (p.188).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of ENTP and ENFP.** An egocentric desire to "stabilize their outer life in some unconditional way." They demand "unconditional support from others" and test people's limits to force proof of love (p.206). Jake expected others to supply assignments and deadlines but felt "used and controlled" when those didn't serve his goals (p.102). ENTPs forget to eat, live on junk food, and run down (p.213). At midlife ENFPs become "abruptly aware of the progression of time" and yearn for "a lasting investment, invulnerable to chance" (p.218).
- **As the tertiary of INTP and INFP.** INTPs focus defensively on health, food, and environment (tomatoes plus dairy "poisonous"), and physical symptoms mirror emotional states (pp.302, 315). INFPs avoid situations that force compromise, narrow their world, and develop idiosyncratic preferences others must accommodate (pp.388, 402).

**Stories and metaphors**

- The beggar: "So? You had a bad week, and I should suffer?" ESJs see an ingrate; ISJs understand him (p.175).

### 3. Extraverted Intuition (Ne): right hemisphere, front. Ch 16, pp.196–221. Dominant in ENTP and ENFP; secondary in INTP and INFP; tertiary in ESTJ and ESFJ; inferior in ISTJ and ISFJ.

**Definition.** Ne is right-brain pattern recognition aimed at context. It grasps a few cues as a whole pattern, the way you recognize a tune or a face, and immediately sees options that don't exist yet. It is less "the big picture" as an overview than a fast, future-facing grasp of what a situation could become. It feels like knowledge even when it is wrong (pp.196–198). Socially, it champions potential and becomes "the herald of a better way" (p.203).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Infers a whole from one or two cues (sorting mail by return address; recognizing a rented movie from its first scenes) (p.198).
2. Forms strong "gut" impressions of people from resemblance to past patterns, sometimes wrongly (pp.197–198).
3. An informed generalist who can hold their own in conversation on many topics (p.201).
4. Gets swept up by a new possibility, "falling in love," unable to talk about anything else (p.202).
5. Loses interest once the idea's value is clear to others or partly realized (pp.203–204).
6. Develops a hunch by recruiting feedback and collaborators rather than working it out on paper first (the chaos-theory analyst who built a conference series in six months) (p.202).
7. Generates new options at the moment of decision and resists ranking one option as inherently better (p.200; Clinton and the lawn, pp.215–216).
8. Overcommits, treating intentions as "as good as realized"; may not start until the deadline has passed (p.205).
9. Restless and bored without change, new prospects, or room for improvement (p.201).
10. Prefers jobs with variety and turnover (journalism, PR, emergency medicine) and changes direction often (pp.50, 203).
11. Forgets physical needs when absorbed (p.213).
12. Hands follow-through and detail to supporters (pp.204, 211).

**What it is not**
- **Not "intuitive" in the casual sense** of natural talent or women's intuition (pp.199–201).
- **Not Ni, its "mirror image" across hemispheres:**
  - The ENP physician believes the disease pattern "was always there, waiting for someone to discover it" and wants to tell everyone. For INJs patterns are "part of us," useful but arbitrary constructs (pp.224–225).
  - ENPs picture wholeness as an integrated endpoint (global village, unified theory). For INJs wholeness is "the chaotic beginning" (p.230).
  - "ENPs are most visible in the first flush of discovery … INJs are least accessible in the discovery process" (p.230).
- **Not Se.** Se is concrete and pragmatic and becomes the focal point of others' expectations (the evangelist in The Apostle). Ne appeals to imagination and inventiveness (p.202). Se moves on when the excitement is gone; Ne moves on "before anything of consequence has even happened" (p.203).
- **Not commitment-phobia as a value.** Ne types are "consistently surprised by the passionate responses they inspire" (p.204).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of ISTJ and ISFJ.** ISJs "become genuinely cautious." They suspect others want them to accept changes that compromise their integrity and that those people lack discipline. "Inferior Intuition persuades them that the larger picture is at stake, and that accepting the change will collapse the whole structure" (p.182).
- **As the tertiary of ESTJ and ESFJ.** Grant's runaway daydreams on used-car lots: "When was it going to be his turn?" (p.94). ESTJs' tertiary Ne focuses on "negative possibilities," worries about others' weakness, and keeps people dependent (p.279). ESFJs are "overwhelmed by the need to change things … right now, at any cost … behind their backs if necessary" (p.355). When healthy it gives ESFJs well-roundedness, adolescent play, and the ability to see that traditional values look different in different contexts (p.355).
- **As the dominant under stress.** ENFPs feel "tired and overwhelmed … too much seems to be coming at them," because "they're constantly changing in response to their circumstances" (p.220).

**Stories and metaphors**
- The panther in the jungle versus the panther in the zoo (p.197).
- The jukebox selecting a 45 (p.198).
- Name That Tune (p.198).
- Stavrogin in Dostoyevsky's The Devils: "Don't repeat my old ideas to me" (pp.203–204).
- Seekers versus Companions (p.204).

- Odan the Trill, who changes host bodies; ENP politicians do the same (pp.206–208).
- Worf accepting dishonor, showing what secondary Fi gives an ENFP (pp.218–219).
- The Five Easy Pieces hitchhiker's Alaska: "I think that was before the Big Thaw" (p.218).
- Jim Carrey: "starved for affection when I'm making love to my ideas" (p.211).
- The ENTP scientist who "seemed like the kind of guy you'd go bowling with" (p.211).
- Types: Steve Jobs, Deepak Chopra, Gingrich (ENTP); Bill Clinton (ENFP).

**Key quotes**

### 4. Introverted Intuition (Ni): left hemisphere, back. Ch 17, pp.222–250. Dominant in INTJ and INFJ; secondary in ENTJ and ENFJ; tertiary in ISTP and ISFP; inferior in ESTP and ESFP.

**Definition.** Ni attends to perception itself. It is aware that the same information can be framed many ways, and that labels and categories decide what we see. It "liberates" impressions from their context to create new options for perceiving. It is left-brain and language-oriented: it works at the edge of existing vocabulary and pushes past it. Truth is a frame of reference; reframing changes options (pp.222–225, 228).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Solves a dilemma by redefining the problem rather than choosing within its terms (Kirk reprogramming the no-win simulation; the INFJ editor's "add the s" solution) (pp.225–226).
2. Notices how words, labels, and categories shape assumptions and is drawn to editing, terminology, linguistics, theology, and math (pp.228–229).
3. In the middle of an experience, wonders why people hold a convention (the beach-tan question) and expects others to think they are observing instead of enjoying (pp.222–223).
4. The more they learn, the more they feel must be known before acting (p.231).
5. Develops insights privately and resists involving others until the insight fits words (the Cinderella slipper) (p.230).
6. Answers a logical argument by pointing out the speaker's need to see it that way (p.234).
7. Is fascinated by things they can't explain, and loses interest once the thing becomes recognized or popular (Green Man carvings; the character actor who won an Emmy) (pp.231–232).
8. Without external limits, doesn't know how much to say and over-delivers (pp.68, 229).
9. Neglects practical upkeep: dentist, bills, insurance (p.232).
10. Uses critique of others' ideas as the main contribution (the INTJ seminary professor) (p.231).
11. Imposes causal logic on fiction (quantum parallel timelines for Dallas) (pp.226–227).
12. Enjoys physical activity but treats it as "another conceptual viewpoint" (working on a moving van "to make room for a little body consciousness") (p.232).

**What it is not**
- **Not hunches or feelings.** "The conceptual nature of Introverted Intuition may be difficult to appreciate" (p.223).
- **Not Ne** (see Ne above).
- **Not Si.** ISJs secure the structure of public information; "INJs are interested in changing or improving it" (p.224).
- **Not Te, even in INTJs.** INTJs only "superficially resemble Extraverted Thinkers": "if the theory works, use it. But don't believe it" (p.240). ENTJs want a better mousetrap, INTJs question whether mice should be caught (implied, p.265). ENTJs pursue goals compatible with reason, while INTJs "will steal fire from the gods without any assurance that a reasonable hearth exists" (p.240).
- **Not Fe-driven sociability in INFJs.** INFJs are "receptive to others only up to a point," and their sympathy is mistaken for friendship overtures (p.246).
- **Not "psychic."** Sensate pop culture stereotypes INFJs as neurotic psychics and INTJs as nerds or absent-minded professors (p.232).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of ESTP and ESFP.** Ni "suggests that absolute meaning is an illusion." ESPs experience it as criticism "from the standpoint of someone's abstract assumptions—someone who hasn't been there." They feel discriminated against for their surface identity (p.153). Society-wide it shows as "magical thinking" and "suspicion of conspiracy where none exists" (pp.155–156). ESTPs feel restless and dissatisfied (p.162); ESFPs feel depression and confusion (pp.166–167).
- **As the tertiary of ISTP and ISFP.** ISTPs see "institutional hypocrisy" everywhere, the system hiding the truth, with "a slightly paranoid quality," and attract "the disenfranchised and the iconoclast" (pp.303, 310). ISFPs pursue an alternate lifestyle that embodies their critique and use flip defensiveness against being co-opted (pp.388, 395). The later-seasons X-Files Mulder is "more like an ISP influenced by tertiary Introverted Intuition" (p.233).
- **As the dominant under stress.** Their vision becomes "a psychological castle and they stand in the highest parapet, warning people that they aren't worthy to come in." They become "oracles" who only point out others' poverty (pp.237–238).

**Stories and metaphors**
- The beach: Se lies in the sun, Si packs extra towels and a watch, Ne hears music and imagines an amusement park, Ni asks why tans are attractive (pp.222–223).
- The fussy-baby reframe from "difficult" to "vivid, strong" (p.224).
- "That chick with the green face" (p.226).
- The surgeon's-sister riddle (p.228).
- "Trains at the edge of articulated knowledge … put on a hat, grab hold of a boxcar door" (p.229).
- "Cup Intuition's small flame against the hard wind" (p.229).
- Charles Williams's "everlasting dance" (p.230); the Internet as INJ thinking (pp.230–231).
- Spock: "infinite diversity in infinite variations" (p.231).
- INJs using judgment like light against the Denevan parasites, exposing ideas as arbitrary (pp.233–234).
- Scully versus Mulder as Ahab: "Scully, are you coming on to me?" (p.233).
- "After ecstasy, the laundry" (p.245).
- INFJ as Merlin called by Nimue's song (p.250).
- The INFJ boy who answered an order to mow the lawn with a brilliant poem about coercion (p.249).

- Types: Dennis Miller, George Carlin (INJ humor); Mulder (INTJ); Richard Pryor's edge (INFJ).

**Key quotes**

### 5. Extraverted Thinking (Te): left hemisphere, front. Ch 18, pp.253–285. Dominant in ESTJ and ENTJ; secondary in ISTJ and INTJ; tertiary in ESFP and ENFP; inferior in ISFP and INFP.

**Definition.** Te organizes experience by impersonal, shared standards of order: sequence, cause and effect, hierarchy, category, measurement. It pulls a principle out of one context so it can be applied to new ones ("portable ghosts"). Its standards are mostly collective, and their "truth" lies in "rational utility," not scientific accuracy. The result is common benchmarks that free us from the dictates of immediate experience (pp.253–258).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Organizes by impersonal schemes (alphabet, calendar, rank, category) rather than by relationship (pp.254, 318).
2. Asked about one part of a problem, explains the whole linear process from the beginning (p.265).
3. Sets goals and plans even for leisure, then reviews afterward to prepare for next time ("retrodiction") (p.265).
4. Treats something as real only if it can be measured or proved (p.265).
5. Chooses the principled option even when an exception would clearly benefit them (the ESTJ who took a 9% fixed mortgage for a two-year stay) (p.262).
6. Sees mitigating circumstances as excuses (p.262).
7. Expresses involvement through critique and problem description, and is surprised when others read analysis as anger. Rarely compliments the normal course of things (p.269).
8. Sees no need to repeat a commitment once stated (p.269).
9. Justifies leisure in purposeful terms ("getting some exercise") (p.269).
10. Dislikes interruption mid-project and may redo earlier steps to regain their place (ESTJ, p.277).
11. Thinks hierarchically: standing should be earned step by step (p.275).
12. Plans a trip in advance and is frustrated when reality deviates (p.277).
13. ENTJ: compelled to point out an illogical premise or wasted effort; hates saying anything twice (p.281).

**What it is not**
- **Not the only kind of "Thinking."** Verbal, abstract logic is Te specifically; Ti thinks "by way of visual, tactile, or spatial logic" (p.74).
- **Not cold or emotionless.** "Throughout history, Thinking types have been willing to die for the sake of their principles … Thinkers differ … in their registration of emotional effects" (p.267). Displays of feeling feel like "playacting" to them (p.268).
- **Not Fe.** Both are left-brain, rational, and collective. Te principles are "portable ghosts" that transfer across contexts. "Feeling standards are not portable ghosts … They consist of ritualized behaviors" (p.266). Tevye weighs "on the one hand / on the other hand," while Golde has "no 'other hands'" (pp.326–327).
- **Not Ti** (see Ti below: baseball rules versus rounding second).
- **Not control for its own sake.** Te types "are so accustomed to putting aside their immediate interests for the sake of their principles that they lose sight of their own needs" (p.262).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of ISFP and INFP.** IFPs feel they've "compromised their values for the sake of others' structural priorities," "zero in on the boundaries" to eliminate them, and are surprised when others feel threatened (p.385). They become stalemated (p.386). INFPs turn passive-aggressive: they "drag their feet" or "'yes' people, then do as they like" (p.401). They procrastinate or do unnecessary tasks (p.400). They turn values into "a left-brain system of classification" of good and bad people (p.388). Example: the vegetarian-preaching INFP minister who was fired (p.388).
- **As the tertiary of ESFP and ENFP.** ESFPs grow "susceptible to popular religious, political, or psychological theories" and become "players in a new arena" (p.167). ENFPs judge critics as "negative, controlling," insist others respect their priorities, and find it hard to apologize for a missed appointment (pp.208, 220–221). Jake used tertiary Te to prove others "narrow-minded or manipulative" (pp.102–103). When healthy it helps them "set logical priorities" (p.220).

**Stories and metaphors**
- The spoon dropped from a high chair a thousand times (p.255).
- Letterman pushing watermelons off buildings (pp.255–256).
- Principles as "ghosts in the mind" (p.256).
- The prisoner scratching a calendar into the wall (p.257).
- The INTJ seminarian's Pharisees as "micromanagers" seeking structural integrity (p.258).
- A rock collection sorted many ways, each favoring a different rock (pp.259–260).
- The Fahrenheit 451 "living books" (p.264).
- Janeway applying Federation rules to a lost ship (p.263).
- Quark's dilemma: dying to honor the Rules of Acquisition (pp.263–264).
- Surveyors needing to "get into the water and swim" (p.270).
- The Last Wave: "the Law is greater than just man" (p.273).
- ESTJs see "people … like buildings: The deeper the foundation, the higher they can go" (p.275).
- Sherlock Holmes (p.274).
- The ENTJ professor who never gave an A+ (p.281).

**Key quotes**

### 6. Introverted Thinking (Ti): right hemisphere, back. Ch 19, pp.286–316. Dominant in ISTP and INTP; secondary in ESTP and ENTP; tertiary in ISFJ and INFJ; inferior in ESFJ and ENFJ.

**Definition.** Ti is impersonal logic applied to the live situation. It coordinates action with many variables at once in light of a goal. It is "body based and wholistic," works through visual, tactile, and spatial cues, and operates tacitly in the background of skilled action. It reasons by probability and leaves room for the random, but it requires hands-on engagement (pp.286–288, 290). In INTPs it becomes an interest in the active relationship between a pattern and its environment, explored through models (p.294).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Packs groceries by eyeballing the space rather than measuring (p.287).
2. Wants a diagram of the finished assembly rather than step-by-step instructions (p.287).
3. Learns music by ear or by grasping structure, not through scales and notation (p.306).
4. Makes split-second decisions that balance many variables toward a goal ("rounding second base") (p.287).
5. Has trouble explaining how they knew; experiences their reasoning as instinct or "the zone" (pp.288, 290–291).
6. Is either fully "with" a situation or not, and won't fake interest, explain, or apologize (p.308).
7. Contributes where they can affect the logical outcome rather than through social gestures (the INTP architect who called the doctors instead of sitting at the bedside) (p.293).
8. Masters one craft deeply and is content to divide time between one or two areas of expertise (pp.299, 301).
9. Prefers freelancing or independence to institutional loyalty (p.307).
10. Intensely private in some settings, open and generous with like-minded people ("like a clam with a broken hinge") (pp.293–294).
11. Explores ideas through models, designs, or prototypes (INTP) (p.311).
12. Finds abstract classroom reasoning tedious but excels hands-on (shop, music, gym) (pp.295–296).

**What it is not**
- **Not Te.** "We know the rules of the game by way of Extraverted Thinking … But let's say it's the top of the ninth … rounding second base … What we want is subjective logic" (pp.286–287). ETJs "make a firm split between observer and observed"; ITPs are "part of the process, changing its nature by changing themselves" (pp.292–293).
- **Not mere impulsivity or reflex.** "Thinking is always discriminating and logical, whether it's Extraverted or Introverted" (p.306).
- **Not the "action-junkie SP" pattern.** ISTPs don't need novelty: "Once they're using familiar skills, every situation is new" (p.307). This is her argument against Keirsey (pp.289–291).
- **Not Fi.** Ti is "dispassionate and impersonal … systemic logic"; Fi gauges an evolving pattern against "an experiential ideal" (p.366).
- **Not Fe-free.** Ti people do have feelings, but "experience their feelings only in the course of using their subjective logic" (p.309).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of ESFJ and ENFJ.** Thomson writes: "The impassive curiosity it fosters strikes EFJs as cold and inhuman, tantamount to pulling the wings off flies" (p.343). EFJs then project it as others' "selfishness … poor judgment." They give unsolicited advice and set up rules to "guard people against dangerous influences," trying to control every variable (pp.343–344). They become jealous and possessive and talk constantly about their sacrifices: "does anyone thank them? No. They get heartache, grief, ulcers" (pp.344–346). They strain at gnats, such as the ESFJ sending birthday gifts only to her stepfather's biological children (pp.353–355).
- **As the tertiary of ISFJ and INFJ.** ISFJs feel their actions are part of "a much larger scheme whose integrity must be maintained self-sacrificially." They show loyalty to lost causes and avoid intimacy (p.195). INJs decide that truth is an inexpressible core and need no investment in the outer world (p.237).
- **As the dominant under stress.** ITPs' "slightly paranoid" convictions combine with indifference: "the idea that people are trying to make them look bad" (Taxi Driver's "You talkin' to me?") (p.303).

**Stories and metaphors**
- Hammering a nail as the model of "tacit" information (p.290).
- Pirsig on steel: "Steel can be any shape you want if you are skilled enough" (p.292).
- ITPs as alchemists in harmony with fluid patterns (p.292).
- Frank Lloyd Wright's leaky roofs (p.295).
- Einstein as an average student (p.295).

- The TWA 800 coroner adjusting his methods to the families' tacit input (p.298).

- "You hadda be there" (p.307).
- The INTP mathematician who ran singles parties as a strategic game (p.300).

### 7. Extraverted Feeling (Fe): left hemisphere, front. Ch 20, pp.317–364. Dominant in ESFJ and ENFJ; secondary in ISFJ and INFJ; tertiary in ESTP and ENTP; inferior in ISTP and INTP.

**Definition.** Fe is rational, left-brain, objective reasoning by relatedness. It organizes people and obligations by category of relationship (family, friend, coworker) and reads and uses the shared social vocabulary of gestures, rituals, and customs that make relationships visible and predictable. It is not emotional preference. It often overrides personal liking for the sake of social obligation. Its evidence is "systemic, not individual" (pp.318–322).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Ranks contacts and priorities by closeness of relationship (family first, then friends, then coworkers) (p.318).
2. Matches help to the type of relationship: sympathy for the store clerk whose cellar flooded, mop and pail for the best friend (p.320).
3. Includes people their relationship role requires, whatever their private feelings (the stepmother at family gatherings) (p.321).
4. Decides by talking it over: seeks advice, consensus, and feedback (pp.331, 351).
5. Needs background facts (hometown, family, work) before feeling able to relate to someone (p.331).
6. Remembers birthdays and children's ages, makes sure everyone at a gathering feels included, helps clean up (p.332).
7. Reads meaning into expected gestures and is hurt by their absence (the land in Texas instead of a diamond ring) (p.332).
8. Softens criticism with a polite disclaimer and expects it to be acknowledged (p.45).
9. Steers a group away from open conflict, often by reframing positively (pp.359–360).
10. Finds it hard to say no and becomes overextended (pp.340, 359).
11. Hears "do you really believe that?" as a challenge to the relationship (pp.335–336).
12. Feels guilty about negative private thoughts and tries to eliminate them (p.340).
13. ESFJ: may not know where they stand until learning where others stand (pp.352–353).

**What it is not**
- **Not emotion or sentiment.** "It's entirely possible to use this language strategically, without 'feeling' anything at all" (p.267). Extreme Fe "leads to anything but a reliance on emotion" (p.321).
- **Not Fi.** "You've been like a mother to me" presumes shared standards (Fe); Fi reasons from "fundamental human values, whose meaning … [is] conditioned by our own experience" (p.216). They "produce inverse results": Fe relies on custom and law to cover decisions that affect the community; Fi relies on experience and empathy to cover decisions that affect us as human beings (p.370).
- **Not Te.** See Fiddler on the Roof: Tzeitel goes to Tevye, not Golde, because Thinking is flexible (pp.325–327).
- **Not small talk.** EFJs "are no more tolerant than Thinking types of 'idle chitchat'" (p.331).

### 8. Introverted Feeling (Fi): right hemisphere, back. Ch 21, pp.365–404. Dominant in ISFP and INFP; secondary in ESFP and ENFP; tertiary in ISTJ and INTJ; inferior in ESTJ and ENTJ.

**Definition.** Fi is right-brain, in-the-moment judgment that gauges an unfolding situation against an inner ideal trained by direct experience of good and bad. It is like a camera lens "ground by our personal experience," adjusted for present conditions. It makes things one's own and puts human value ahead of structural, statistical, or status considerations. It is rational but not logical. Thomson stresses the developmental task of distinguishing merely circumstantial or subjective values from unconditional human ones (pp.365–368, 372–373).

**Behavioral markers**
1. Cooks or creates by tasting and adjusting toward a known ideal, not by recipe (p.366).
2. Arranges belongings to reflect what matters to them, trying and changing until it "feels right" (the bookcase for the books they love best) (p.366).
3. Will defy rules or odds to protect a person or a principle of human dignity (Nikita) (pp.367–368).
4. Struggles to explain why something is right: "Being nice is better—because it's nicer" (p.399).
5. "Live and let live" on the surface; firm values underneath (pp.371, 396).
6. Feels responsible for private intentions, not just actions (Carter's "lust in his heart") (p.398).
7. Goes along with outer routines while their "real" self is invested elsewhere (pp.384, 387).
8. Creates personal rituals, altars, or sacred spaces to mark meaningful events (the Brandon Lee altar) (p.378).
9. Disregards status when deciding whom to serve (Mother Teresa passing the water along) (p.382).
10. Feels they're "making do" until their "real life" or purpose begins (p.371).
11. Resists pressure quietly: delays, agrees and then does otherwise (p.401).
12. Drawn to work that crosses boundaries for humane ends (Peace Corps, Doctors without Borders, Habitat) (p.371).
13. Withdraws suddenly to recharge after warm engagement, surprising others (INFP, p.398).

**What it is not**
- **Not emotion, impressionism, or sentimentality.** Its "bottom-rung potential for impressionism and sentimentality" is a stereotype (p.367). The buffalo soldiers' conflict "has nothing to do with sentimentality" (p.369).
- **Not Fe** (see above). Without Fi, Fe becomes the Vorta: they "smile when required, say the right things—but no one ever believes them" (pp.369–370).
- **Not spirituality as such.** "Introverted Feeling is not tantamount to spirituality" (p.380).
- **Not a basis for a social system.** "They don't offer a basis for an objective social system … Introverted Feeling acquires prophetic power only by virtue of its relationship to a framework of common expectations" (pp.380–381).
- **Not Ti.** Ti is impersonal systemic logic, the probable consequences of choice; Fi is "a personal relationship to an evolving pattern" (p.366).
- **ISFP versus INFP.** The core Fi lens is the same; the secondary differs. Se adjusts the lens for concrete changes, Ne for hidden patterns (pp.371–372).

**Inferior or stress expression**
- **As the inferior of ESTJ and ENTJ.** "They believe that other people aren't being logical or responsible enough, obliging them to take control and be the grown-up" (p.270). Loved ones' needs feel disruptive. They become perfectionist and know "the right way and the wrong way to do almost everything." They can't relax or stop supervising (p.271). Grant's self-righteous "lone fighter for justice" stand (p.95). ENTJs feel "controlled by people's needs or expectations" (p.284). The Thinker's version of an argument: "You don't appreciate me!" (p.115).
- **As the tertiary of ISTJ and INTJ.** ISTJs: "quite sure that their ideas about what is important are unconditional," using rules to authorize their impressions, a "martinet" (p.190). ISJs in general become "the only decent, moral, or reasonable person … to maintain the still point at the center of a disintegrating universe" (p.184). INTJs "idealize their abstract ideas about life and … avoid real relationships as unworthy," credited with "staying above the emotional fray … when they have actually never been in it" (p.245). Maggie's dreams felt "cosmically important" (p.108).

### C3. A useful framing for result explanations

Thomson's growth logic is consistent across all 16 types:
1. Over-reliance on the dominant leads to feeling stuck.
2. The inferior is projected onto other people as the problem.
3. The tertiary steps in with a justified fight-or-flight rationalization.
4. Real growth comes from deliberately engaging the secondary, which brings its opposite attitude into play.
5. Once the secondary is engaged, the tertiary turns helpful and the inferior "loses some of its negative leverage" (p.94).

Her core sentences for this arc: "Everyday existence is filled with ordinary conflicts that are really calls to assimilate more of who we are" (p.77), and "This feeling of being stuck is always the give-away" (p.84).
