# PART A. The framework (Jung and Myers side by side)

## A1. The two attitudes (E/I)
- **Jung's criterion is where the deciding value comes from, not sociability.** Extraversion is "when the orientation to the object and to objective facts is so predominant that the most frequent and essential decisions and actions are determined, not by subjective values but by objective relations" (p. 417).
  - His examples: one man wears a coat because it is cold, another doesn't, to harden himself. One admires the tenor because everyone does, another withholds approval because general admiration proves nothing (p. 416).
- **Introversion:** "Introverted consciousness doubtless views the external conditions, but it selects the subjective determinants as the decisive ones" (p. 472). The subjective factor is "the other world-law", as valid as the object (p. 474).
- **The sociable/taciturn picture is only how it looks** to an observer (p. 413). Definitions: Extraversion is "a strong, if not exclusive, determination by the object" (p. 543). Introversion is when "the subject is the chief factor of motivation while the object at most receives only a secondary value" (p. 567).
- **The attitude is innate and spread at random:** "In the same family one child is introverted, and another extraverted" (p. 414). A "falsification of type" under outside pressure tends to produce neurosis and exhaustion (pp. 415–416).
- **Myers puts it more simply:** introverts' main interest is "the inner world of concepts and ideas", extraverts' is "the outer world of people and things". "The natural preference remains, like right- or left-handedness" (p. 7).
  - "The conduct of extraverts is based on the outer situation… The introvert, however, *starts* farther back—with the inner ideas" (p. 53).
  - Her figure: extraverts outnumber introverts about 3 to 1 (p. 54).

## A2. The four functions: rational (judging) vs irrational (perceiving)
- **Jung:** the four functions are "neither mutually relatable nor mutually reducible", and he can give "no a priori reason" for there being exactly four (p. 547).
- **Thinking and feeling are rational.** They "attain their fullest significance when in fullest possible accord with the laws of reason" (p. 584). Feeling counts as rational because "values in general are bestowed according to the laws of reason" (p. 545).
- **Sensation and intuition are irrational.** "Irrational" means "not… contrary to reason, but something outside the province of reason" (p. 569). These functions "aim at pure perception" and so "entirely forgo rational direction" (pp. 570–571, 584).
- **Irrational types are not unreasonable:** "They are merely in a high degree empirical… their judgment cannot keep pace with their experience" (p. 468).
- **Each kind misjudges the other.** The irrational type sees the rational as "something only half alive, whose only aim in life consists in fastening the fetters of reason upon everything living" (p. 469).
- **Myers' version:** perceiving means "becoming aware of things, people, occurrences, and ideas". Judging means "coming to conclusions about what has been perceived" (p. 1).
  - "A reader who considers first whether they are consistent and logical is using thinking judgment. A reader who is conscious first that the ideas are pleasing or displeasing, supporting or threatening ideas already prized, is using feeling judgment" (p. 3).
  - Her everyday test: waking to rain and thinking "Why, it's raining hard" is perception; "I'd better check if it's raining in!" is judgment (p. 191).

## A3. Dominant, auxiliary, inferior
- **Jung, one sovereign function:** "This absolute sovereignty always belongs, empirically, to one function alone, and can belong only to one function," because adaptation needs "constantly clear and unambiguous aims" (p. 514).
- **Jung, the auxiliary:** "In conjunction with the most differentiated function, another function of secondary importance… is constantly present, and is a relatively determining factor" (p. 513).
  - It must be "different from, though not antagonistic to" the leading function: thinking pairs with intuition or sensation, "never with feeling" (p. 515).
  - "For all the types appearing in practice… there is also a relatively unconscious, auxiliary function which is in every respect different from the nature of the main function" (p. 515).
  - Typical pairings (p. 515–516): "the practical intellect… paired with sensation, the speculative intellect breaking through with intuition, the artistic intuition which selects and presents its images by means of feeling judgment, the philosophical intuition which, in league with a vigorous intellect, translates its vision into… comprehensible thought."
- **Jung, the inferior function:** "the function that remains in arrear in the process of differentiation" (p. 563).
  - Society "enforce[s] a man to apply himself first and foremost to the differentiation of that function with which he is either most gifted by Nature, or which provides his most effective means for social success" (p. 564).
  - It is inferior "in the psychological, though not in the psycho-pathological, sense… merely backward." It "normally remains conscious" and sinks into the unconscious only in neurosis (p. 564).
  - "The superior function is always the expression of the conscious personality, its aim, its will, and its achievement, whilst the inferior functions belong to the things that happen to one" (p. 426).
  - "The unconscious functions are in an archaic, animal state" (p. 517).
- **Jung, development path:** grow toward the inferior *through the auxiliary*. Pushing the inferior directly "must always come to grief" (p. 516).
- **Jung, the third function:** for a "conscious practical intellect" (thinking + sensation) the unconscious is "intuitive-feeling", with feeling "relatively stronger" inhibited than intuition (p. 516). This implies a ranking of third over fourth.
- **Myers, one function in charge:** "a ship needs a captain with undisputed authority" (pp. 9–10). "One process—sensing, intuition, thinking, or feeling—must have clear sovereignty" (p. 11).
- **Myers, the auxiliary:** "adequate (but by no means equal) development of a second process, not as a rival… but as a welcome auxiliary… An extreme perceptive with no judgment is all sail and no rudder. An extreme judging type with no perception is all form and no content" (p. 11).
- **Myers, General and Aide:** in the extravert "the General is always out in the open". In the introvert "the General is inside the tent… It is the Aide whom others meet" (p. 13).
  - "A cardinal precaution in dealing with introverts… is not to assume, just from ordinary contact, that they have revealed what really matters to them" (p. 14).
- **Myers, the two lesser processes:** they are like "younger members of the family". If ignored they are "like slaves in a dungeon… they eventually break out and come up to consciousness in violent revolt" (p. 194). "Even in effective adults, the two least-used processes remain relatively childish" (p. 175).
- **Myers, the shadow:** "the product of the least-developed part, which a person rejects and disowns… 'I don't know how I came to do that. I didn't mean to!' are usually the work of the shadow… The shadow is something that happens when a person isn't looking" (p. 84).

## A4. "Good type development" (Myers, Ch. 16 and 19)
- **Two conditions:** (1) "adequate but by no means equal development of a judging process and a perceptive process, one of which predominates". (2) "adequate but by no means equal facility in using both the extraverted and introverted attitudes" (p. 174).
- **Balance is not equality:** "*balance* does not refer to equality of two processes or of two attitudes; instead, it means superior skill in one, supplemented by a helpful but not competitive skill in the other." "Perception without judgment is spineless; judgment with no perception is blind. Introversion lacking any extraversion is impractical; extraversion with no introversion is superficial" (p. 174).
- **Choosing is required:** developing S and N at once is "like listening to two radio stations on the same wave length" (p. 174).
- **Final stage:** only after mastering the top two do people "cultivate the values of the previously neglected third and fourth processes… ultimately transcend their type." Tried too early, this "may merely divert the person" (p. 168).
- **Development is a variable:** "a variable with a wide range and profound influence on effectiveness, success, happiness, and mental health… can *substitute for intelligence*" (p. 177). "Good type development can be achieved at any age" (p. 191).
- **Appropriate vs inappropriate use of each process (p. 178):**
  - Appropriate: sensing for "seeing and facing the facts"; intuition for "seeing a possibility and bringing it to pass"; thinking for "analyzing the probable consequences"; feeling for "considering what matters most to oneself and others".
  - Inappropriate: "indulging sensing by running away from a problem to a trivial amusement, giving in to intuition by dreaming up impossibilities that would provide an effortless solution, indulging feeling judgment by rehearsing how right and blameless one has been all along, and yielding to thinking judgment by criticizing anyone who has an opposite view."
- **Judgment belongs on yourself:** "When should a judging type not use judgment? … When dealing with other people… For use on other people, perception is fairer, kinder, and more productive" (p. 105). "Judgment should be used on one's own concerns" (p. 195).

## A5. Preferences, not abilities (Myers)
- "These basic differences concern the way people *prefer* to use their minds" (p. 1). "Most people, from infancy up, *enjoy* one more than the other" (p. 2).
- "How much excellence people actually achieve depends in part on their energy and their aspirations, but… *the kind of excellence toward which they are headed* is determined by the inborn preferences" (p. 8).
- **Sensing is not lower intelligence:** "It would be grossly mistaken but easy to conclude that sensing types are less 'intelligent'"; tests are "couched in the intuitive's language" (p. 59). The intuition–education link "may be as much in interest as aptitude" (p. 58).
- **Thinking is not a monopoly:** "it should never be assumed that thinkers have a monopoly on all worthwhile mental activity. They do not even have a monopoly on thinking"; INFP and INFJ have some of the highest scholarship records (p. 66). "The mark of a thinker is not so much the possession of greater mental powers but having them run on a different track" (p. 67).
- **Strengths are relative:** "Jones is not merely weak where Smith is strong: Jones is also strong where Smith is weak" (p. 115). "No type has everything" (p. 118). "No one has to be good at everything" (p. 201).
- **Everyone has all four:** "The four processes… are gifts that all people are born with" (p. 193).
- **Labels are not boxes:** "To describe people as ENTPs does not infringe on their right to self-determination: They have already exercised this right by preferring" (p. 9).

# PART B. Quote bank for "it's a spectrum" storytelling

1. Jung, p. 10: "every individual possesses both mechanisms extraversion as well as introversion, and only the relative predominance of the one or the other determines the type."
2. Jung, p. 13: "there can never occur a pure type in the sense that he is entirely possessed of the one mechanism with a complete atrophy of the other. A typical attitude always signifies the merely relative predominance of one mechanism."
3. Jung, p. 13: "A rhythmical alternation of both forms of psychic activity may correspond with the normal course of life."
4. Jung, p. 513: "I have no desire to give my readers the impression that such pure types occur at all frequently in actual practice. They are, as it were, only Galtonesque family-portraits, which sum up in a cumulative image the common and therefore typical characters, stressing these disproportionately, while the individual features are just as disproportionately effaced."
5. Jung, p. 426: "A normal extraverted attitude does not, of course, mean that the individual behaves invariably in accordance with the extraverted schema… A habitus can be called extraverted only when the mechanism of extraversion predominates."
6. Jung, p. 436, on extraverted thinkers: "We have now outlined two extreme figures, between which terminals the majority of these types may be graduated."
7. Jung, p. 496: a perfectly reasonable judgment "would presuppose a uniform development of both extraversion and introversion… Under ordinary circumstances, therefore, an ideal reason is impossible."
8. Jung, p. 433: "both orientations are one-sided, with a definitely restricted validity; hence they both require this mutual correction."
9. Jung, p. 10: "it is often a difficult matter to discover to which type an individual belongs, especially when oneself is in question. Judgment in relation to one's own personality is indeed always extraordinarily clouded."
10. Myers, p. 7: "This is not to say that anyone is limited either to the inner world or to the outer… the natural preference remains, like right- or left-handedness."
11. Myers, p. 8: "There is a time to perceive and a time to judge… Both attitudes have merit."
12. Myers, p. 3: "Most people would agree that they make some decisions with thinking and some with feeling."
13. Myers, p. 77: "the traits that result from each preference do not combine to influence an individual's personality by simple *addition* of characteristics; instead, the traits result from the interaction of the preferences."
14. Myers, p. 84: "The descriptions are designed to apply to each type at its best… Actually the types come in widely different states of development."
15. Myers, p. 177: "type development is a variable with a wide range."

A counterweight to keep in mind, so the site doesn't oversell "you're balanced". Jung says that when thinking and feeling are equal "there is also no question of a differentiated type, but merely of a relatively undeveloped thinking and feeling. Uniform consciousness and unconsciousness of functions is, therefore, a distinguishing mark of a primitive mentality" (pp. 514–515). Myers repeats it: "Some people dislike the idea of a dominant process and prefer to think of themselves as using all four processes equally. However, Jung holds that such impartiality, where it actually exists, keeps all of the processes relatively undeveloped" (p. 11).

So the honest spectrum story has two parts. Everyone uses all eight, and pure types don't exist. But a clear lead function is the normal, healthy pattern, and a flat profile is not automatically "well-rounded".

# PART C. The eight function-attitudes

Marker tags: [J] is Jung, [M] is Myers, [J+M] is both. Markers are phrased neutrally so they can become items.

## 1. Te: Extraverted Thinking

**Definition.** Thinking whose standard of truth comes from outside the self: objective facts, or generally accepted ideas taken from tradition, education or science. It turns conclusions into rules and plans that organize the outer world, including other people.
- Jung's test for whether thinking is extraverted: "by which standard is its judgment governed—is it furnished from without, or is its origin subjective?" (pp. 428–429).

**Behavioral markers**
- **Runs things.** Uses thinking "to run as much of the world as may be theirs to run"; enjoys "deciding what ought to be done and giving the appropriate orders" [M p. 85].
- **Can't stand inefficiency.** Abhors "confusion, inefficiency, half measures, anything that is aimless and ineffective" [M p. 85].
- **Rules for everyone, self included.** Builds "a code of rules embodying their basic judgments about the world. They aim to live by those rules, and consider that others should as well" [M p. 86].
  - Speech is heavy on "One really should" and "one must" [J p. 436].
- **Decides fast, even outside its expertise.** "The almost irresistible urge to *make* decisions, just for their own sake… quick but *faulty* decisions in a strange field" [M p. 86].
- **Plans and schedules.** Sets objectives in advance and works systematically to hit them on schedule; holds own conduct to the same standard [M pp. 85–86].
- **Builds as it critiques.** Its judgment is "synthetic… even when it analyses, it constructs"; it "always substitutes a fresh value for one that is demolished" [J p. 442].
- **Cuts the personal out of decisions.** Rules out personal considerations, the way a judge or surgeon does [M p. 67]. Truthful over tactful when forced to choose [M p. 68].
- **Measures everything against one formula.** Good, bad, even beauty are judged by "the formula", and exceptions are not tolerated [J p. 435].
- **Can be convinced by reasoning.** "Extraverted thinkers are convinced by reasoning, and when they are convinced… when they decide to do something, it gets done" [M p. 87].
- **Neglects personal life when the goal takes over.** "His health is neglected… often the most vital interests of his family are violated" [J p. 439]. Emotional life is "accidental"; social life is "incidental" [M p. 85].

**Confusions: what Te is not**
- **Not "thinking about concrete things".** "It is no proof of its extraverted nature that it is preoccupied with concrete objects" (p. 429). Te can be "purely ideal thinking" if its ideas are "borrowed from without… by tradition and education" (p. 428). The criterion is where the standard comes from, not concrete vs abstract.
- **Not "better logic" than Ti.** The orientation "changes nothing as regards the logic of thought" (p. 431).
- **Each thinking type sees the worst in the other.** Ti looks "quite arbitrary" to Te; Te looks "altogether dull and banal" to Ti (pp. 432–433).
- **Thinking that follows another function is not Te.** It "simply mimics the dominant function, supporting it with arguments that unmistakably contradict the laws of logic" (p. 443). The "I have understood it—I can reconstruct it" kind of remark is non-dominant thinking (p. 443).
- **Not the same as being bossy or a J-letter.** Myers' Fe-dominant types also "like to have things decided and settled" (p. 92).

**When feeling is inferior (the Te-dominant's shadow)**
- Repressed feeling takes "aesthetic activities, taste, artistic sense, the art of friendship" with it [J p. 437].
- Unconscious feelings are "highly personal and oversensitive", producing "a decided readiness to misconstrue any objective opposition to his formula as personal ill-will" [J p. 439].
- Tone becomes "sharp, pointed, aggressive"; feelings have "an untimely and halting character, which is always a mark of the inferior function… a pronounced tendency to resentment" [J p. 439]. They are "petty, suspicious, crossgrained, and conservative" [J p. 440].
- Conscious altruism is "crossed by a secret self-seeking". The end comes to justify the means [J p. 438].
- The view hardens into dogma: "fanaticism, after all, is merely overcompensated doubt" [J p. 441].
- Family pays the price: "his own children… only know such a father as a cruel tyrant, whilst the outer world resounds with the fame of his humanity" [J p. 439].
- Myers: if feeling is suppressed too long, it may embarrass them "by unexpected explosions of temper which consciously they would never 'think' of committing" (p. 87). The rule code can "become a tyranny" for those around them, "especially their families" (p. 86).

**Key quotes**
- "By this formula are good and evil measured, and beauty and ugliness determined" (Jung p. 435).
- "If the formula is wide enough, this type may play a very useful rôle in social life, either as a reformer… or as the propagator of important innovations. But the more rigid the formula, the more does he develop into a grumbler, a crafty reasoner, and a self-righteous critic" (Jung p. 436).
- "The basic mistake here is the infliction of one's own judgment on other people" (Myers p. 86).

## 2. Ti: Introverted Thinking

**Definition.** Thinking whose deciding standard is an internal sense of how ideas must fit together, a "subjective feeling of direction" or inner model. It "begins in the subject, and returns to the subject" (p. 480). It aims at theories, principles and clarity rather than at managing outside facts.

**Behavioral markers**
- **Asks questions and builds theories.** "It formulates questions and creates theories; it opens up prospects and yields insight, but in the presence of facts it exhibits a reserved demeanour" [J p. 481].
- **Facts serve the idea.** "Facts are collected as evidence or examples for a theory, but never for their own sake" [J p. 481; M p. 91].
  - Myers' professor gave a "B" because the student stressed facts over principle (p. 91).
- **Analyzes rather than runs.** "Introverted thinkers use their thinking to analyze the world, not to run it" [M p. 88]. Decisiveness "usually shows only in intellectual matters" [M p. 88].
- **Easygoing until a principle is broken.** "Detachedly curious and quite adaptable—until one of their ruling principles is violated, at which point they stop adapting" [M p. 89].
- **Over-qualifies.** Writing is "loaded and complicated by all sorts of accessories, qualifications, saving clauses, doubts" [J p. 487]. Feels bound to "state the exact truth, with every qualification" even for a simple question [M p. 91].
- **Assumes clarity is shared.** "Only with difficulty can he persuade himself to admit that what is clear to him may not be equally clear to everyone" [J p. 487].
- **Doesn't sell its ideas.** "He merely exposes them, and is often extremely annoyed when they fail to thrive on their own account"; "hardly ever will he go out of his way to win anyone's appreciation of it" [J p. 486; quoted by M p. 89].
- **Cares about coherence over outcome.** "I don't care *what* the truth turns out to be—but I want it to hang together" [M p. 65, quoting a 17-year-old introverted thinker].
- **Slow, scrupulous, stubborn.** "His work goes slowly and with difficulty"; "stubborn, head-strong, and quite unamenable to influence" in pursuit of ideas [J pp. 486–487].
- **Improves on acquaintance.** Seems "prickly, inaccessible, haughty" from afar, but "the better one knows him, the more favourable one's judgment becomes" [J pp. 487–488]. Shy when young; poor at small talk [M p. 88].

**Confusions: what Ti is not**
- **Not "abstract" by definition.** It "may be conceived either with concrete or with abstract factors" (p. 480). ISTP applies it to mechanics and statistics (Myers p. 90).
- **Not idle association.** "A simple stringing together of representations… associative thinking… is not thinking at all" (p. 611).
- **Undirected brainstorming is intuition.** "Passive thinking" is "intellectual intuition… an irrational function" (pp. 611–612). Myers adds that thinking "does not include *all* ingenious mental activity, much of which, in fact, is the product of intuition" (p. 192). Being clever with ideas does not mean Ti.
- **Ti vs Fi.** Both are introverted, can look cold, and depend on an inner image. "Everything… said of the introverted thinking refers equally to introverted feeling, only here everything is felt while there it was thought" (p. 490). Thoughts are easier to put into words than feelings are.

**When feeling is inferior (the Ti-dominant's shadow)**
- Inferior feeling is "primitive" and extraverted: "a primitive feeling, to which objects attach themselves with magical force" [J p. 491]. The other lesser functions also have "a primitive, extraverted character" [J p. 489].
- "His suggestibility to personal influences is in strange contrast" to his intellectual stubbornness. He lets himself be "exploited in the most ignominious way, if only he can be left undisturbed"; "if he should ever chance to be understood, he is credulously liable to overestimate" [J pp. 486–487].
- Puts on a "mask of urbanity" that clashes with his real nature [J p. 486]. Can be "seduced by his primitive affects into biting and fruitless polemics" [J p. 487].
- When the type intensifies: "venomous and personal retorts against every criticism, however just", plus bitterness and isolation [J p. 488].
- Myers: "They are not apt to know, unless told, what matters emotionally to another person." Advice: praise when it's due, and name agreements before disagreements (p. 89).

**Key quotes**
- "Intensity is his aim, not extensity" (Jung p. 485).
- "Its goal is to see how external facts fit into, and fulfil, the framework of the idea" (Jung p. 481).
- "This thinking easily loses itself in the immense truth of the subjective factor. It creates theories for the sake of theories" (Jung p. 482).

## 3. Fe: Extraverted Feeling

**Definition.** Valuing that is tuned to the situation and to shared, collective standards: what is fitting, harmonious and appropriate between people. Its aim is to create and keep good feeling-relations with others.
- "Feeling in the extraverted attitude is orientated by objective data… It agrees with objective values" (p. 446).

**Behavioral markers**
- **Gives the response the setting calls for.** Calls the host's painting "beautiful" because it fits the moment and keeps the feeling-atmosphere pleasant: "not… a simulation or a lie—it is merely an act of accommodation" [J p. 446].
- **Joins shared occasions.** Turns up at the theatre, concerts or church "with correctly adjusted positive feelings"; supports social, philanthropic and cultural causes [J p. 447].
- **Knows what to say.** "Able almost always to express the feelings appropriate to the moment" [M p. 92].
- **Seeks harmony and expects it to be reachable.** "Have faith that harmony can somehow be achieved and often manage to bring it about" [M p. 92].
- **Thinks out loud with people.** "Their thoughts take shape while being expressed"; "they think best when talking with people"; they spend a lot of time in meetings [M p. 93].
- **Sensitive to approval.** Warmed by approval, "sensitive to indifference", "anxious to conform to all legitimate expectations" [M p. 92].
- **Checks how others feel before having an opinion.** The ENFJ said: "I didn't know what to tell her, because I didn't know how she felt about it" [M p. 95].
- **Settled values.** Regards "the world as a place where most of the decisions have already been made"; values feel "self-evident" and come with "an impulse to express" them [M p. 93].
- **Avoids hard facts.** Finds it "harder… than for other types to look squarely at things that they wish were not true"; may "ignore their problems instead of finding good solutions" [M p. 94].
- **Looks for what to admire.** Tends to "concentrate on a person's most admirable qualities" [M p. 92]. Tactful over truthful when forced to choose [M p. 68].

**Confusions: what Fe is not**
- **Not fake.** Jung insists Fe's judgments are "genuine" (pp. 446, 449). Items must not frame Fe as people-pleasing dishonesty.
- **Not emotionality.** Feeling is "distinguished from affect" because it gives rise to "no perceptible physical innervations" (p. 544). Myers: "Feeling should not be confused with emotions" (p. 192).
- **Fe vs Fi.** Fe "adapts the individual to the objective situation". Fi "adapts the objective situation to the individual by… excluding or ignoring the unacceptable". Fe is "extensive rather than deep"; Fi is "deep and passionate rather than extensive" [M Fig. 29, p. 79].
  - Facing an unpleasant fact: "extraverted feeling denies its existence, and introverted feeling condemns its existence" [M p. 93].
- **INFJ vs Fe-dominant.** INFJs can "appear extraverted… It is actually the feeling process, not the individual, that is extraverted" [M p. 112].

**When thinking is inferior (the Fe-dominant's shadow)**
- "What she cannot feel, she cannot consciously think… every conclusion, however logical, that might lead to a disturbance of feeling is rejected from the outset" [J p. 449].
  - Jung's patient: "But I can't think what I don't feel" [J p. 449].
- The repressed thinking is "infantile, archaic, and negative" [J p. 451]. It surfaces in "irruptions, often of an obsessing nature… negative and depreciatory": "moments when the most hideous thoughts fasten upon the very objects most valued by their feelings" [J p. 452].
  - Its style is the "nothing but" reduction, e.g. "How much does he make by it?" [J pp. 444, 452].
- An extraverted feeling type "enjoys an excellent feeling rapport with his entourage, yet occasionally opinions of an incomparable tactlessness will happen to him" [J pp. 426–427].
- Overdone: "extravagant demonstrations of feeling… They ring hollow", and the person may flip to "an entirely contrary estimation of the selfsame object" [J p. 450].
- Myers: without good perception they "adopt the *forms* of feeling judgment that the community sanctions" and "jump to conclusions" (p. 94).

**Key quotes**
- "Without this feeling, for instance, a beautiful and harmonious sociability would be unthinkable" (Jung p. 447).
- "It is feeling which serves as the bridge between one human being and another" (Myers p. 66).
- "Value, above all, harmonious human contacts" (Myers p. 92).

## 4. Fi: Introverted Feeling

**Definition.** Valuing measured against an inner, often wordless ideal ("an image which has no existence in reality, but of which it has had a sort of previous vision", p. 490). It ranks what matters by a private hierarchy, is felt intensely rather than shown widely, and is not aimed at influencing others.

**Behavioral markers**
- **Reserved until close.** "Have feelings that are deep but seldom expressed, because inner tenderness and passionate conviction are both masked by reserve and repose" [M p. 95]. "They wear their warm side inside, like a fur-lined coat" [M p. 96].
- **Doesn't try to change people.** Displays "a sympathetic parallelism, which has no desire to affect others, either to impress, influence, or change them in any way" [J p. 492]. "No desire to impress or influence others" [M p. 95].
- **Follows its own code.** "Maintain independence from the judgment of others, being bound by inner moral law"; "keeping all lesser values subordinate to the greater" [M p. 95].
- **Flexible until a core value is hit.** "Open-minded, flexible, and adaptable—until one of the things they value most deeply seems in danger: Then they stop adapting" [M p. 96]. Myers' perceptive-looking student was voted "most decisive" once her values were touched (p. 73).
- **Needs work to mean something.** "They want to have a purpose behind their paycheck, no matter how big the check"; perfectionist when feeling is engaged; prefers individual work [M p. 96].
- **Cools strong emotion around it.** Others' strong emotions get "damped and rebuffed… 'cooled off'"; sweeping enthusiasm meets "a benevolent neutrality" [J pp. 492–493].
- **Distant with strangers, warm with intimates.** "The unfamiliar object is shown no touch of amiability" [J p. 492].
- **Expresses feeling indirectly.** Through art, writing, poetry or private devotion rather than direct display: "often prefer the *written* word… without making personal contact" (INFP) [M p. 98; J p. 494].
- **Sympathy shows as stillness.** "Intensive sympathy… gains a passionate depth… To the outer world… this sympathy looks like coldness, for it does nothing visibly" [J pp. 493–494].
- **Underrates itself.** ISFP is "the most modest type" [M p. 98]. Vulnerable to "a sense of impotence and inferiority" when the real falls short of the ideal [M p. 96].

**Confusions: what Fi is not**
- **Not selfishness.** Labels like "philautic, egocentric… are both objectionable and out of place, since they foster the prejudice that it is invariably a question of the beloved ego" (p. 474). Fi turns egocentric only when "falsified by an egocentric attitude" (p. 491).
- **Not the absence of feeling.** "A superficial judgment might well be betrayed, by a rather cold and reserved demeanour, into denying all feeling to this type. Such a view, however, would be quite false; the truth is, her feelings are intensive rather than extensive" (p. 493).
- **Not "I express my emotions".** Fi often scores low on expressiveness items, so expressiveness should not be used to measure it.
- **Fi vs Fe:** see Fe above, and Myers Fig. 29 (p. 79).

**When thinking is inferior (the Fi-dominant's shadow)**
- Fi "is counterbalanced by a primitive thinking, whose concretism and slavery to facts passes all bounds" [J p. 491].
- When the ego takes over: "Consciousness begins to 'feel what others think'… others are thinking all sorts of baseness, scheming evil… the subject must also begin to carry out preventive intrigues, to suspect and sound others"; "innumerable secret rivalries develop"; the person ends up exhausted [J p. 495].
- Fi's hidden intensity can leak out as "a domineering influence often difficult to define… a sort of stifling or oppressive feeling" [J p. 494].
- Myers: "feeling types are never logical enough to estimate the full cost of something that appeals to them" (p. 120). Without an outlet: "living upon sentiment, illusion, and self-pity" [M Fig. 29, p. 79].

**Key quotes**
- "It is a feeling which apparently depreciates the object; hence it usually becomes noticeable in its negative manifestations" (Jung p. 490).
- "It makes men silent and difficult of access; with the sensitiveness of the mimosa, it shrinks from the brutality of the object, in order to expand into the depths of the subject" (Jung p. 490).
- "The feeling type need only say, 'This is valuable to me.'" (Myers p. 67).

## 5. Se: Extraverted Sensation

**Definition.** Perception that takes in concrete reality as it is, now, at full strength. The most vivid stimulus wins its attention. It values actual, tangible experience and enjoyment over theory or interpretation.
- "Its sole criterion of value is the strength of the sensation as conditioned by its objective qualities" (p. 457).

**Behavioral markers**
- **Sees things as they are.** "Feeling dominant… see things as they 'should' be… thinking dominant… as they logically 'must' be… intuition dominant… as they can be made to be; but the extraverted sensing types… see things as they are" [M p. 99].
- **Adapts to what's in front of it.** "They never fight the facts… If what they have started to do is blocked, they do it another way. They will follow no plan that has ceased to fit the circumstances" [M p. 99].
- **Enjoys quality.** Values "concrete enjoyment, from good food and good clothes to music, art, the beauties of nature" [M p. 100]. "He dresses well… keeps a good table for his friends" [J p. 459].
- **Curious about new sensory things, wary of abstract ones.** Interested in "new food, scenery, people, activities, objects, gadgets"; things that can't be grasped by the senses "seem less real" [M p. 100].
- **Hands-on with machines and physical skills.** Instinctive "affinity for machinery and a sure sense of what it can and cannot be made to do"; likes sport and exercise [M p. 100].
- **Learns by doing.** "Learn most and best from experience, making a better showing in life than in school"; studying is "memory work" [M pp. 99–100].
- **Moves on to the next experience.** Uses experience "to serve as a guide to fresh sensations"; "the more pronounced he is, the less use does he make of his experience" [J pp. 457–458].
- **Explains things physically.** Readily blames a mood on the weather: "will… relate an obvious psychogenic symptom to the falling barometer, while the existence of a psychic conflict seems to him a fantastic abnormality" [J p. 459].
- **Acts more than talks.** ESTPs "prefer action to conversation" [M p. 101]. Good at "pulling conflicting factions together and making things run smoothly" [M pp. 99–100].
- **Lives in the present.** Prefers "the art of living in the present to the satisfactions of enterprise and achievement"; "most reluctant to sacrifice present enjoyment to future gain" [M Fig. 25, p. 63].

**Confusions: what Se is not**
- **Not crude hedonism.** "It by no means follows that he is just sensual or gross, for he may differentiate his sensation to the finest pitch of aesthetic purity" [J p. 458]. He can be "a jolly fellow, and often a refined aesthete" [J p. 458].
- **Se vs Ne in practice.** In the extraverted attitude intuition "comes very near to sensation". The difference is whether the strongest physical stimulus leads, or a sensation selected by unconscious expectation and used as a "directing-point" for distant vision [J pp. 462–463].
- **Not "detail" as such.** Introverted sensing types are also "conspicuous for… application to detail" [M p. 102]. The difference is present-tense outer intensity vs inner impression.
- **Not being rational.** Se can look rational because it is realistic, "but this is by no means the case" [J p. 458].

**When intuition is inferior (the Se-dominant's shadow)**
- "The repressed intuitions begin to assert themselves in the form of projections upon the object. The strangest conjectures arise; in the case of a sexual object, jealous phantasies and anxiety-states play a great rôle. More acute cases develop every sort of phobia" [J p. 460].
- "Absurdly scrupulous morality coupled with a primitive, superstitious and magical religiosity"; intuition shrinks to "a sniffing into every corner; instead of searching the horizon, it recedes to the narrowest gauge of human meanness" [J p. 460].
- Myers: ES types are "most prone to *drift* into wrongdoing, from a thoughtless yielding to circumstances or bad companions" when they lack insight and judgment (p. 173). Without judgment there is a "danger of laziness, instability, and a generally shallow personality" (p. 101).

**Key quotes**
- "No other human type can equal the extraverted sensation-type in realism" (Jung p. 457).
- "His aim is concrete enjoyment… His ideal is the actual" (Jung pp. 458–459).
- "Are endowed with a great capacity for enjoying life and a zest for experience of all kinds" (Myers p. 99).

## 6. Si: Introverted Sensation

**Definition.** Sense perception in which the inner impression an object leaves matters more than the object itself. The person sees the same things as everyone else but responds to a vivid, personal and often unpredictable layer of impression.
- "The subject perceives the same things as everybody else, only he never stops at the purely objective effect, but concerns himself with the subjective perception released by the objective stimulus" (p. 499).

**Behavioral markers**
- **Calm outside, busy inside.** "Outwardly matter-of-fact, inwardly entertained by extremely individual reactions to their sense impressions" [M p. 102].
  - Endnote: "Really, it is a two-way conversation… Only what I say isn't out loud" [M p. 204, n. 10].
- **Unexpected private associations.** A "vivid private reaction" that shows only "off duty", and may be "absurd, irreverent, touching, or hilarious, but never predictable" [M p. 103].
- **Hard to predict what will strike it.** "Judging from without… it is practically impossible to foretell what will make an impression and what will not" [J p. 501]. Myers: "impossible to predict what outer stimulus will catch and hold attention" [Fig. 30, p. 80].
- **Looks calm and passive.** May "stand out by the very calmness and passivity of his demeanour, or by his rational self-control" [J p. 501].
- **Evens things out.** "The too-low is raised a little, the too-high is made a little lower; the enthusiastic is damped, the extravagant restrained and the unusual brought within the 'correct formula'" [J pp. 502–503].
- **Builds firm views from stored impressions.** Ideas rest on "a deep, solid accumulation of stored impressions, which gives them some almost unshakable ideas"; "habitually compare present and past situations" [M p. 102].
- **Steady and thorough.** "Systematic, painstaking, and thorough… patient and willing application to detail… excellent adaptation to routine" [M p. 102]. "They do not enter into things impulsively, but once in, they are very hard to distract, discourage, or stop" [M p. 102].
- **Reads carefully, distrusts quick answers.** "Do not trust an answer that suddenly appears… They will not skim in reading" [M p. 59]. The ISFJ reads each test question "three or four times" [M p. 59].
- **Wants it stated plainly.** "Like everything kept factual and stated clearly and simply" [M p. 103]. Annoyed "when you leave things to their imagination" [M p. 59].
- **Puts up with misuse, then pushes back hard.** "Allow themselves to be abused, for which they usually take vengeance at the most unsuitable occasions with redoubled stubbornness and resistance" [J p. 503].

**Confusions: what Si is not**
- **Not memory or tradition at its core.** Jung's Si is about the subjective impression: "Subjective sensation apprehends the background of the physical world rather than its surface" (p. 500).
  - His illustration is painters rendering the same landscape differently (p. 498). It sees things "as a million-year old consciousness might see them" (p. 500).
  - Memory and past-comparison come from Myers (pp. 60, 102, 104), and "conservative, valuing custom and convention" is a trait Myers gives to the *extraverted* sensing types (p. 99).
  - Recommendation: don't make "I like tradition/routine" the main Si marker. Use "my inner impression of things is vivid and personal" plus "I compare with past experience".
- **Si vs Ni, Jung's sharpest contrast (pp. 505–506).** During a bout of dizziness, sensation is "arrested by the peculiar character of this innervation-disturbance, perceiving all its qualities, its intensity, its transient course… without raising the smallest inquiry concerning the nature of the thing which produced the disturbance". Intuition "peers behind the scenes… sees the image of a tottering man pierced through the heart by an arrow".
- **Si's calm can pass for a rational type** [J p. 501].
- **Si vs Se:** Se follows "the strongest stimulus"; Si's attention is "very selective, guided wholly by the inner constellation of interests" [M Fig. 30, p. 80].

**When intuition is inferior (the Si-dominant's shadow)**
- Repressed intuition becomes extraverted and archaic, with "an amazing flair for every ambiguous, gloomy, dirty, and dangerous possibility in the background of reality… something actually undermining, which often stands in most vivid contrast to the gentle benevolence of consciousness" [J p. 504]. It produces "compulsive ideas" [J p. 504].
- Myers, for sensing types who shut intuition out: "dislike the sudden and unexpected because their security rests on knowing from experience what to do. When something outside their experience threatens to happen, they can only give up and let it happen or else fight it blindly" (p. 195).
- Undeveloped: "absorbed in subjective reactions to sensory impressions and lacking outlets" [M p. 105]. Communication shrinks to "conventional banalities about the weather" [M Fig. 30, p. 80].

**Key quotes**
- "Introverted sensation conveys an image whose effect is not so much to reproduce the object as to throw over it a wrapping whose lustre is derived from age-old subjective experience" (Jung p. 500).
- "They look on tempests and are never shaken" (Myers p. 102).
- "Absorb and enjoy using an immense number of facts" (Myers p. 102).

## 7. Ne: Extraverted Intuition

**Definition.** Perception of possibilities in the outer world: what a situation, person or thing could become. Present facts serve as springboards. Once a possibility is realized or pinned down, interest moves on.
- "A fact is acknowledged only in so far as it opens up fresh possibilities of advancing beyond it" (p. 464).

**Behavioral markers**
- **Spots early potential.** "He has a keen nose for things in the bud pregnant with future promise" [J p. 464].
- **Feels trapped by settled situations.** "Every ordinary situation in life seems like a closed room, which intuition has to open… In a very short time every actual situation becomes a prison" [J p. 463]. "Stable conditions have an air of impending suffocation" [J p. 464].
- **Strong starts, weaker finishes.** "Strong in initiative and creative impulse, but not so strong in completing projects"; "lives… likely to be a succession of projects" [M pp. 105–106].
- **Drops a project once it's proven.** Once "the genie" is out of the bottle, "it does not interest them anymore… Somebody else can take over" [M p. 106]. Jung: "others reap the harvest" [J p. 466].
- **Driven by bursts of energy.** "Operate by impulsive energy rather than concentrated will-power"; "tireless at what interests them, but find it hard to get other things done" [M p. 106]. In service of a possibility "they may forget to eat or sleep" [M p. 106].
- **Hates routine** [M p. 106]. "Abhor the routine because it leaves nothing for inspiration to accomplish" [M p. 58].
- **Reads people's potential and inspires them.** "Capacity… to make an intuitive diagnosis of their abilities"; "to inspire his fellow-men with courage, or to kindle enthusiasm for something new, is unrivalled, although he may have forsworn it by the morrow" [J p. 466].
  - Myers: teachers who "divine the unguessed potentials" (p. 108).
- **Gets energized by obstacles.** "Stimulated by difficulties and most ingenious in solving them" [M p. 106]. "Are alert to all the possibilities" [M p. 105].
- **Backs underdogs with promise.** "The natural advocate of every minority that holds the seed of future promise" [J p. 466].
- **Reads between the lines.** Intuitive readers "read between and beyond the lines to the possibilities that come to mind" [M p. 2]. They are "imaginative at the expense of observation" [M Fig. 25, p. 63].

**Confusions: what Ne is not**
- **Not "ignoring the senses".** The intuitive "actually has sensations, but he is not guided by them per se, merely using them as directing-points for his distant vision". If asked, he describes things "quite indistinguishable from sense-perceptions" [J pp. 462–463]. Self-reports about "noticing things" are therefore ambiguous.
- **Moving on is not fickleness.** "To call this loss of interest fickle, as the judging types are apt to do, is to miss the point"; intuitives are "as stubbornly loyal to their guiding principle, the inspiring possibility, as the sensing types are to the facts" [M p. 107].
- **Ne vs Ni:** Ne "uses the inner understanding in the interests of the objective situation"; Ni "uses the objective situation in the interests of the inner understanding". Ne finds self-expression "natural and easy", Ni finds it "difficult". Both share "habitual expectancy" and "quick understanding" [M Fig. 31, p. 81].
- **Not intelligence.** Intuitives define intelligence as "quickness of understanding" and "so prejudge the case in their own favor" [M p. 59].

**When sensation is inferior (the Ne-dominant's shadow)**
- Unconscious sensation brings "suspicions of approaching illness"; "a sudden entanglement with a most unsuitable woman [or man]… an unconsciously compelling tie to an object of incontestable futility" [J p. 467].
- "He rids himself of the restrictions of reason, only to fall a victim to unconscious neurotic compulsions in the form of oversubtle, negative reasoning, hair-splitting dialectics, and a compulsive tie to the sensation of the object" [J p. 467].
- "For this oblivion the object sooner or later takes revenge in the form of hypochondriacal, compulsive ideas, phobias, and every imaginable kind of absurd bodily sensation" [J p. 467]. Jung adds an ethical shadow: "consideration for the welfare of his neighbours is weak" [J p. 465].
- Myers: without judgment they "do not finish things… are unstable, undependable, easily discouraged… do not do anything that they do not want to do" (pp. 107–108). The temptation is "to *assume* that an attractive possibility… is as possible as it looks" (p. 92).

**Key quotes**
- "Emerging possibilities are compelling motives from which intuition cannot escape and to which all else must be sacrificed" (Jung p. 464; quoted by Myers p. 106).
- "He spends himself animating men and things, spreading around him an abundance of life—a life, however, which others live, not he" (Jung p. 466).
- "The common factor in all these manifestations of intuition is a sort of ski jump—a soaring take-off from the known and established, ending in a swooping arrival at an advanced point, with the intervening steps apparently left out" (Myers p. 57).

## 8. Ni: Introverted Intuition

**Definition.** Intuition aimed at the inner world: it perceives the images, patterns and meanings stirring behind experience. Its conclusions arrive whole, with strong conviction, and are hard to explain or communicate.
- "Although this intuition may receive its impetus from outer objects, it is never arrested by the external possibilities, but stays with that factor which the outer object releases within" (p. 505).

**Behavioral markers**
- **Guided by an inner vision.** "Are driven by their inner vision of the possibilities"; "determined to the point of stubbornness" [M p. 109].
- **Sure without being able to say why.** Intuition presents content "as a complete whole, without our being able to explain or discover in what way this content has been arrived at", with "an intrinsic character of certainty and conviction" [J p. 568].
  - Myers: their "utter conviction of their intuition's validity makes them impervious to the influence of outside judgment" (p. 110).
- **Struggles to put insight into words.** "Find self-expression difficult" [M Fig. 31, p. 81]. Jung: "His language is not that which is commonly spoken—it becomes too subjective. His argument lacks convincing reason. He can only confess or pronounce" [J p. 510].
- **Can't be pushed.** "These people cannot be successfully coerced. They will not even be *told* anything without their permission, but they will accept an offer of facts, opinions, or theories, for free consideration" [M p. 111].
- **Looks ahead.** "Can even foresee new possibilities in more or less clear outline, as well as the event which later actually transpires" [J p. 508].
- **Prefers the untried route.** "More interested in pioneering a new road than in anything to be found along the beaten path"; "the impossible takes a little longer—but not much" [M p. 109].
- **Indifferent to the body and practical life.** "The consciousness of his own bodily existence fades from the introverted intuitive's view" [J p. 507]. To outsiders, "Reality has no existence for him" [J p. 507].
- **Looks for meaning.** Myers: "insight into the deeper meanings of things" (p. 109). Jung's moral variant asks: "What does this mean for me and for the world?" [J p. 509].
- **Shares in fragments.** Communication is "fragmentary and… episodic", and the manner can seem "brusque, repelling", of which "they are quite unaware" [J p. 511].
- **Restless in routine.** "Deeply discontented in a routine job that offers no scope for inspiration" [M p. 109]. INTJ: "Whatever is, could doubtless be improved!" [M p. 111].

**Confusions: what Ni is not**
- **Ni vs Ne:** see the Ne section and Myers Fig. 31 (p. 81). Ne pursues outer possibilities; Ni pursues new angles "for viewing and understanding life".
- **Ni vs Si:** Jung's dizziness example (pp. 505–506; see Si above).
- **Ni vs Ti/Fi:** an intuitive who "stops at perception" (aesthetic) differs from one who adds judgment (moral) [J p. 509]. Myers: introverted intuitives "must develop" a judging process or they have "little or no development of an outer personality" (p. 110).
- **Not mysticism by default.** Jung's "mystical dreamer and seer… fantastical crank and artist" (p. 508) are extreme portraits. Myers' INTJ and INFJ are effective "research scientists and design engineers", battalion commanders and student presidents (p. 110).

**When sensation is inferior (the Ni-dominant's shadow)**
- "The unconscious personality may, therefore, best be described as an extraverted sensation-type of a rather low and primitive order. Impulsiveness and unrestraint are the characters of this sensation, combined with an extraordinary dependence upon the sense impression" [J p. 510].
- When this turns into open opposition: "compulsive sensations… partly hypochondriacal manifestations, partly hypersensibility of the sense organs and partly compulsive ties to definite persons or other objects" [J p. 510].
- In everyday terms, the risk is overlooking facts and their limits. Intuitives may assume "that there are no facts to be known on the point in question, or that they already know all the facts" [M p. 195].

**Key quotes**
- "Just as the extraverted intuitive is continually scenting out new possibilities… so the introverted intuitive moves from image to image, chasing after every possibility in the teeming womb of the unconscious, without establishing any connection between the phenomenon and himself" (Jung p. 507).
- "Had this type not existed, there would have been no prophets in Israel" (Jung p. 507).
- "Are willing to concede that the impossible takes a little longer—but not much" (Myers p. 109).

# PART D. What makes a function measurable, and what self-report gets wrong

1. **Surface items are "straws", not the essence.** Myers: "many trivial reactions are useful for identification, but these are merely straws to show which way the wind blows. They are not the wind. It would be a mistake to assume that the essence of an attitude or of a perceptive or judging process is defined by its trivial surface effects or by the test items that reflect it" (p. 23). Use several kinds of item per function (values, process, behavior, reactions) so no single surface habit decides the score.

2. **"Should" vs "do" vs "naturally tend to".** Myers p. 73: readers struggle "because of an inconsistency between what they feel they should do, what they actually do, and what they naturally tend to do. It is the natural tendency that reveals the basic preference. A person's idea of what is right may be an acquired ideal, borrowed from another type, and the person's actual behavior may reflect a somewhat uncongenial good habit learned from parents or accepted because of the person's own dogged efforts."
   - Item design: ask what is effortless or enjoyable, or what you did as a child or do when relaxed. Avoid what you value or think is right. This also covers your "values vs function" worry, since Fi/Fe or Ti/Te items about valuing honesty, harmony or logic pick up ideals, not process.

3. **Social desirability and cultural pressure.**
   - Myers p. 182: "Western civilization has inclined men toward thinking, women toward feeling, and both sexes toward extraversion and the judging attitude… anyone who came into the world as a clean slate would be likely to be marked ESTJ or ESFJ." This predicts inflated E, J, Te/Fe and S-ish self-reports, and gendered T/F answers.
   - Myers p. 66: TF "is the only one that shows a marked sex difference", and atypical people "have often learned the art of protective coloration."
   - Myers p. 182, "Lack of faith in one's own type": rarer types, especially introverted intuitives, at "about one in sixteen" in school years, may stop trusting their preferences.
   - Jung p. 497: introverts often "force [themselves] to join in the extraverted overvaluation of the object"; he blames the extraverted "style of the epoch" (pp. 497–498).
   - Jung p. 564: the most developed function may be the one that "provides his most effective means for social success." Environment shapes it, not just disposition.

4. **Falsified or trained type.** Jung (pp. 415–416, quoted by Myers p. 181): external pressure can produce a "falsification of type". Myers calls the result "inferior, frustrated copies of other people" (p. 181). A strong but joyless skill can look like a preference. Pair skill items ("I can…") with energy or enjoyment items ("it drains / energizes me").

5. **Introverts' dominant is hidden, even from themselves.**
   - What others see, and much of what introverts report about their outward behavior, is the auxiliary: "Introverts whose dominant process is a judging process… do not *outwardly* act like judging people" (Myers p. 13).
   - "The rationality of the introverted feeling type… is too interior and subtle for the observer to perceive with any certainty, *or even for the subject to report*" (Myers p. 22).
   - Item design: for the introverted functions, ask about inner process and what happens "when something important to you is at stake" (Myers pp. 13–14, 73), not everyday outward conduct.

6. **Extraverted dominants are over-visible.** Perception traits are "most evident in the EP types, whose perceptive process is both *extraverted and dominant*… When the perceptive process is merely the auxiliary… its manifestations tend to be more moderate" (p. 62). Judgment traits are likewise "most evident in the EJ types" (p. 67). Raw totals will favor extraverted dominants, so consider normalizing within attitude.

7. **The shadow pollutes both self-report and observer report.**
   - Myers p. 84: "If a person's apparent preferences indicate a given type, yet he or she has behaved in a way wholly alien to the type… If it was inferior to the person's usual standard, there may have been a shadow at work."
   - Myers pp. 84–85: a wife rating her "extremely introverted ISFJ husband" made him ISTJ, "missing the feeling he does not express, and reporting instead the unconscious, inferior, and critical thinking of his shadow side." Stress-time behavior items will mis-type people, so either avoid them or score them as inferior-function markers.

8. **Who you are depends on who's looking.** Jung p. 427: "a judging observer will tend to seize the conscious character, while a perceptive observer will be influenced more by the unconscious character." To decide, observe "which function is placed under the control and motivation of consciousness, and which functions have an accidental and spontaneous character" (pp. 427–428).
   - Good item axis: deliberate and controllable vs it just happens to me (Jung p. 426: superior is "aim, will… achievement", inferior is "things that happen to one").

9. **Jung's basis for self-report, with a caveat.** He bases typing "upon what the individual feels to be his conscious psychology… he is the only competent judge, since he alone knows his own motives" (pp. 453–454). Yet "Judgment in relation to one's own personality is indeed always extraordinarily clouded", and compensation creates "secondary characters… extraordinarily hard to decipher" (p. 10).

10. **Mix-ups between functions that items must avoid.**
    - **Feeling vs emotion:** feeling is not affect (Jung p. 544; Myers p. 192). Don't measure F with "I'm emotional" or "I cry easily".
    - **Feeling-led reasoning that looks like thinking:** "Thought dependent upon feeling… the laws of logic are only ostensibly present" (Jung p. 612). F types "enlist their thinking to find the logical reasons needed to win a thinker's acceptance of a conclusion they have already reached by way of feeling" (Myers p. 66). "I enjoy debating logically" isn't clean T. Ask instead what decides it when logic and values conflict, and what you do first.
    - **Thinking that copies the dominant:** subordinate thinking "simply mimics the dominant function" (Jung p. 443).
    - **Cleverness is often intuition:** Myers p. 192; Jung p. 611 ("intuitive thinking").
    - **Concrete vs abstract is not E vs I:** Jung p. 429.
    - **Intuitives report "sensations":** Jung pp. 462–463.
    - **Undifferentiated functions are blended:** "feeling-sensation"; "confounding hatred with love"; "every positive brings with it an equally strong negative" (pp. 539, 544). Contradictory answers within one function may mean it is undeveloped, not that the person is balanced.

11. **Consistency as a development signal.** Myers pp. 169–171 used split-half reliability as a group-level index of type development. SN and TF reliabilities were lower in less mature groups, while EI and JP stabilize earlier: "perception and judgment should be harder to develop than mere attitudes." You could show a "clarity" or confidence index per axis, and treat low within-function consistency as "less differentiated", not "opposite".

12. **Preference vs ability vs intelligence.** Myers pp. 58–59, 66–67. Don't use difficulty, aptitude or IQ-style items. Speed-based items also favor intuitives (p. 59).

13. **How each type misreads the others.** Thinkers "are no judges of feeling. They naturally judge all feeling by their own" (Myers p. 65). Intuitives underrate sensing perception, and vice versa (Myers pp. 115–116). Jung p. 432: each thinking type sees only the other's "aspect, not its nature". Peer-rating features will inherit these biases.

14. **Jung's sex-linked claims are not usable as data.** Te is "chiefly found among men" (p. 441); Fe and Fi types are almost always women (pp. 448, 492); Ne "favour[s] women" (p. 465). Myers p. 66 frames the TF sex difference as stereotype and "protective coloration". Don't build gendered items or norms on Jung's statements.

# PART E. Jung vs Myers differences that matter for scoring

1. **Attitude of the auxiliary (the big one).**
   - **Myers' rule:** the auxiliary takes the opposite attitude. "If the auxiliary process differs from the dominant process in every respect, it *cannot* be introverted where the dominant process is introverted" (p. 19). Examples: ISTJ = Si + Te; INFP = Fi + Ne (p. 20).
   - **Her evidence from Jung:** p. 515 ("in every respect different"), p. 489 (introverted thinking's lesser functions have "a primitive, extraverted character"), p. 426 (an extravert's inferior functions serve introversion). Plus van der Hoop (endnote 1, p. 203).
   - **Jung himself:** he never states the auxiliary's attitude explicitly. Myers notes "almost all his followers except van der Hoop" assume dominant and auxiliary share the same attitude (pp. 19–21). She calls same-attitude cases "instances of insufficient use and development of the auxiliary" (p. 21).
   - **Scoring implication:** if you score all eight function-attitudes independently (as Sakinorva does), you can show a stack under either model. Myers predicts that the top-2 normally split E/I. Same-attitude top-2s (e.g. Ni + Ti) are, in her view, a development imbalance, not a separate type.

2. **J/P flips for introverts.**
   - J/P shows only the function used "in *dealing with the outside world*" (p. 14). Extravert ending in J: dominant is T or F. Introvert ending in J: dominant is S or N, and the J letter reflects the extraverted *auxiliary* (pp. 14–15, Fig. 1).
   - "In extraverts the last letter describes the General; in introverts it describes the Aide" (endnote 15, p. 204). Example: INTJ = Ni dominant + Te auxiliary.

3. **Rational/irrational vs J/P.**
   - Jung's rational/irrational describes the *dominant* function. Myers' J/P describes the *extraverted* function.
   - Myers finds Jung's split "of little practical use in ascertaining a person's type" because introverted rationality is invisible, and calls "irrational" "a fighting word" (pp. 21–22).
   - J/P "deals only with outward behavior and thus points only indirectly to the dominant process of the introvert" (p. 22). It came from Katharine Briggs' prior typology (p. 22).
   - **Scoring implication:** J/P behaviors (plans, order, closure) are a proxy for whether a judging function is extraverted (Te/Fe high) vs a perceiving one (Se/Ne high). They are not direct measures of Ti, Fi, Si or Ni.

4. **Pure, pathological portraits vs balanced, healthy ones.**
   - Jung describes "the rare, theoretically 'pure' types, who have little or no development of the auxiliary" (Myers p. 17). He was a clinician; most of his inferior-function material is neurosis.
   - Myers writes "to apply to each type at its best" (p. 84). Without the auxiliary, Jung's introverts appear "unable to communicate, to use their insights" (p. 17), which "opens the door for a general misunderstanding… that… the basic extravert-introvert difference [is] a difference in adjustment" (p. 18).
   - **Content implication:** Jung is the source for core definitions and shadow material; Myers is the source for healthy behavioral markers. Rephrase Jung's neurosis language ("hysteria", "psychoasthenia", "compulsion neurosis") into everyday stress language.

5. **8 types vs 16 types.** Considering the auxiliary "splits each of Jung's types into two" (p. 21). Jung gives only seven lines to the combinations (p. 515, quoted by Myers p. 19).

6. **Vocabulary.** Myers says "process" instead of "function" and "sensing" instead of "sensation", avoiding "distracting connotations" (endnote 3, p. 203). "Process" is friendlier for site copy.

7. **The inferior function.**
   - **Jung:** the inferior is the opposite function of the same kind (rational vs rational, irrational vs irrational). It takes the opposite attitude to the dominant, has archaic, compensatory qualities, and erupts when consciousness is one-sided (pp. 426–427, 531–533; "enantiodromia" p. 542).
     - Pairs he describes: Te ↔ inferior Fi (personal, oversensitive feeling); Fe ↔ inferior Ti ("nothing but" negative thinking); Ti ↔ inferior Fe (primitive, magical feeling-ties); Fi ↔ inferior Te (concretistic thinking, slave to facts); Se ↔ inferior Ni; Si ↔ inferior Ne; Ne ↔ inferior Si; Ni ↔ inferior Se.
   - **Myers** uses "least-developed process" and "shadow". She is gentler: integrate the lesser processes as "younger members of the family" (p. 194). She describes developing third and fourth processes only after the top two (p. 168).
   - **Third function:** Jung (p. 516) says the third is less inhibited than the fourth. Neither author, in the chapters read, gives a firm rule for the third function's attitude. Treat any tertiary-attitude rule as a later addition, not something from these books.

8. **Development path.**
   - **Jung:** reach the inferior via the auxiliary, never directly (p. 516). A rational type develops via its irrational auxiliary, and vice versa.
   - **Myers:** master dominant and auxiliary first, then use the opposites by "crossover" in the service of the dominant (pp. 118–121, 196). Her four-step decision exercise is sensing, then intuition, thinking, feeling (p. 197). That exercise is a ready-made "growth tips" section for result pages.
