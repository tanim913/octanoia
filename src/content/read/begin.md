In 1921 the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung published a long, difficult book called *Psychological Types*. He had spent years puzzling over why intelligent, well-meaning people, his patients, his colleagues and he himself, could look at the same situation and come away with completely different accounts of it. His answer was that the mind has more than one way of meeting the world, and that each of us comes to trust some of those ways far more than others.

Octanoia is built on that idea, and on what three later writers made of it. This first chapter gives you the whole map in one place. The chapters that follow walk through each part slowly.

## Two jobs the mind is always doing

Whatever you are doing, your mind is doing two things at once. It is **taking in** information, and it is **deciding** what to make of it.

Jung found two quite different ways of taking things in. **Sensing** registers what is actually there: colours, sounds, facts, the feel of the body, the details of what happened. **Intuition** registers what is not on the surface: patterns, connections, meanings, what something might become. As Isabel Myers put it, sensing tells you *that* something is; intuition reads between and beyond the lines.

He also found two ways of deciding. **Thinking** judges by impersonal logic: is this true, consistent, cause and effect? **Feeling** judges by worth: does this matter, to whom, and how much? Feeling in this sense is not the same as having emotions. It is a reasoned way of weighing values, and it can be just as cool and deliberate as logic.

> Sensation tells us that a thing is. Thinking tells us what the thing is, feeling tells us what it is worth to us.
> — Jung, as quoted by John Beebe, *Energies and Patterns*, ch. 10

## Facing outward, facing inward

Each of those four can work in two directions. Jung called them the two **attitudes**.

An **extraverted** function takes its cue from outside: the situation, other people, shared standards, what is happening now. An **introverted** function takes its cue from an inner reference: a private model, a remembered impression, a felt ideal, an image forming in the background of the mind.

Four functions times two attitudes gives the eight notes Octanoia measures:

- **Se**, extraverted sensing: meeting the world as it is, right now.
- **Si**, introverted sensing: checking the new against a rich store of lived experience.
- **Ne**, extraverted intuition: seeing what a situation could become.
- **Ni**, introverted intuition: sensing the one pattern beneath many things.
- **Te**, extraverted thinking: organising the world by shared, impersonal standards.
- **Ti**, introverted thinking: making things fit an inner model of how they work.
- **Fe**, extraverted feeling: weighing what matters by its effect on people and bonds.
- **Fi**, introverted feeling: measuring things against a personal sense of what is right.

## Everyone plays all eight

This is the point most often lost in popular type talk. Jung never thought there were eight or sixteen kinds of people. He thought there were eight kinds of *consciousness*, and that every person uses all of them. What differs is which ones you reach for first, which ones you trust, and which ones take effort.

> Every individual possesses both mechanisms, extraversion as well as introversion, and only the relative predominance of the one or the other determines the type.
> — Jung, *Psychological Types*, p. 10

Isabel Myers compared it to handedness. You can write with either hand; one simply feels like you. That is why Octanoia talks about notes rather than boxes: everyone plays the whole octave, but each of us has a few notes we return to, and a few we rarely sound.

## The four voices behind Octanoia

**Carl Jung** (*Psychological Types*, 1921) drew the original map: the four functions, the two attitudes, the idea of a leading function and a neglected one.

**Isabel Briggs Myers** (*Gifts Differing*, 1980) made the map practical. She stressed that a leading function needs a helper facing the other way, wrote warm portraits of how each preference looks in daily life, and insisted that preferences are not abilities.

**Lenore Thomson** (*Personality Type: An Owner's Manual*, 1998) gave each of the eight a vivid, concrete character, and described how each type gets stuck and how it grows.

**John Beebe** (*Energies and Patterns in Psychological Type*, 2016) showed that all eight functions play a role in every person, some as trusted allies and some as shadowy characters that appear under pressure.
