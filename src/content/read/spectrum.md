Every chapter so far has described clean shapes: a dominant here, an inferior there. Real people are messier, and all four authors behind Octanoia say so plainly. This chapter is about holding the map lightly.

## No pure types

Jung described his types vividly, but he warned that he was painting family portraits that exaggerate what is typical and leave out what is individual.

> They are, as it were, only Galtonesque family-portraits, which sum up in a cumulative image the common and therefore typical characters, stressing these disproportionately.
> — Jung, *Psychological Types*, p. 513

Myers made the same point about her own type descriptions: they show each type at its best, while actual people come in widely different stages of development. Thomson is blunter: type is a description of, not a prescription for, human behaviour, useful the way wisdom stories are useful.

## Preferences, not abilities

Myers insisted that the functions describe what we *prefer*, not what we *can do*. A sensing type is not less intelligent because intelligence tests reward intuitive quickness; a thinker does not have a monopoly on thinking. Every type is strong where another is weak.

> Jones is not merely weak where Smith is strong: Jones is also strong where Smith is weak.
> — Isabel Myers, *Gifts Differing*, p. 115

## Why your scores might shift

Several things blur the picture, and none of them means the test or you are broken.

**Life stage.** Thomson observes that young adults tend to exaggerate their dominant and underuse their helper, while people in midlife often get close scores everywhere as neglected functions press for attention.

**Circumstances.** A new job, a move, a divorce or retirement can flatten results for a while. So can living closely with someone of a very different type: Thomson describes partners who each carry the other's undeveloped side until both resent it.

**Social pressure.** Myers noticed that Western culture nudges men toward thinking, women toward feeling, and everyone toward extraversion and judgment. People of rarer types sometimes learn to describe themselves in borrowed terms.

**The shadow and stress.** Answers written on a bad week can describe your inferior and shadow functions more than your preferences.

## A flat profile is not the same as balance

There is a tempting reading of close scores: "I'm balanced." Jung and Myers both pushed back. Healthy development, they thought, means a clear lead supported by a helper, not equal use of everything. Myers called it balance in the sense of "superior skill in one, supplemented by a helpful but not competitive skill in the other".

That is why Octanoia reports a *clarity* figure for every function. Low clarity does not mean you are good at both sides; it often means that part of your mind is less settled, or depends heavily on context. Thomson suggests that close scores frequently signal a period of transition rather than a fixed trait.

## What a questionnaire can and cannot do

Beebe points out that even the long-established instruments only agree with each other moderately, and least on thinking versus feeling. The professional ethic around type, he notes, is that the person is the final judge of who they are. A test can only say: this is the pattern your answers suggest; see whether it fits.

Octanoia tries to honour that in three ways. It shows every number with the answers behind it, so you can disagree with a specific reason rather than a verdict. It gives ranked fits instead of one type, and says when two are too close to call. And it keeps your history, so you can watch how your notes move over time.

> Only some such direct experience of the types as one's own, and the permission to consider them in one's own way, can enable a patient to avail himself or herself of the individuation potential of the type theory.
> — John Beebe, *Energies and Patterns*, ch. 3

The best use of your result is as a set of questions: does this describe me? When? What changes when I am stressed, or with certain people? The answers belong to you.
