Two of the four functions take things in. Two decide. This sounds simple, but the distinction explains a great deal about why people misunderstand each other, and it rescues the word *feeling* from a common misreading.

## Perception: what is given

Sensing and intuition are ways of becoming aware of things, people, events and ideas. Jung called them *irrational*, which sounds like an insult but was meant precisely: they are outside the province of reason, not against it. They simply register what is there, without first asking whether it is logical or good.

> They are merely in a high degree empirical … their judgment cannot keep pace with their experience.
> — Jung on the irrational types, *Psychological Types*, p. 468

Beebe likes the phrase *functions of the given*. You do not reason your way to seeing a colour or catching a hunch; it arrives. Myers gave the most everyday example: you wake to rain and think "Why, it's raining hard." That is perception. "I'd better check whether it's raining in" is already judgment.

The two perceiving functions differ in *where* they look. Sensing attends to the actual: the concrete, present, physical, the details. Intuition attends to the possible: patterns, implications, the meaning behind the detail, what is coming. Myers noticed that intuitive readers read between and beyond the lines, while sensing readers notice exactly what the page says.

## Judgment: what to make of it

Thinking and feeling are ways of coming to conclusions about what has been perceived. Jung called them *rational* because both follow laws of reason, even though their laws differ.

Thinking asks whether something is true, consistent, and what follows from what. Feeling asks whether something is worthwhile, fitting, and to whom it matters. Myers put the difference in a reader's first reaction to new ideas: one reader checks whether they are consistent and logical; another notices first whether they are pleasing or displeasing, supporting or threatening what she already prizes.

## Feeling is not emotion

This is the single most important correction in the whole subject. In Jung's sense, feeling is a way of *evaluating*, and it can be calm, exact and principled. Jung distinguished feeling from affect, which shows itself in the body: a racing heart, a flushed face. A judge weighing what a sentence is worth is using feeling; so is a friend deciding, quietly and firmly, what she will not tolerate.

> Feeling, I tell my audiences, is the function that sorts out feelings.
> — John Beebe, *Energies and Patterns*, ch. 2

Thomson goes further: people strongly led by extraverted feeling are often *less* in touch with their own emotions than others, because their attention is on relationships and shared standards. And thinkers are not cold. Plenty have died for their principles; they simply register emotional effects differently.

That is why Octanoia never asks "are you emotional?" to measure feeling, or "do you like abstract ideas?" to measure thinking. Both would measure something else.

## Why the pairing matters

Myers argued that healthy development needs one perceiving and one judging function working together, one leading, one supporting. Perception without judgment is, in her phrase, all sail and no rudder; judgment without perception is all form and no content.

> Perception without judgment is spineless; judgment with no perception is blind.
> — Isabel Myers, *Gifts Differing*, p. 174

She also had a wise piece of advice for people who lead with judgment: use it on your own affairs, and use perception on other people. Perception, she wrote, is fairer, kinder and more productive when applied to others.

## How each kind misreads the other

Jung noticed that perceivers and judgers often find each other baffling. To the perceiver, the strongly rational person can seem half alive, intent on fastening the fetters of reason on everything living. To the judger, the perceiver can seem unreliable, carried along by whatever happens next. Both are seeing only the other's surface. Recognising which kind of process you trust first is the first step towards seeing past it.
