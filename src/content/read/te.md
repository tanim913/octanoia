Extraverted thinking is the part of the mind that builds shared order: plans, rules, measurements, systems that work for everyone. When it is strong, things get done.

## In one breath

Te organises experience by impersonal standards that others can check and agree with: sequence, cause and effect, categories, evidence, results. It turns conclusions into steps and holds everyone, including itself, to the same rules.

> For TJs, freedom isn't the opposite of management. Freedom is management so adept that external reality all but takes care of itself.
> — Lenore Thomson, *Personality Type*, pp. 258–259

## How it works

Jung's test for whether thinking is extraverted is simple: is the standard of judgment furnished from outside, by objective facts or generally accepted ideas, or does it come from within? Te's standard is outside. Its conclusions become a "formula" by which good and bad, even beauty, are measured.

Thomson's image is the child who drops a spoon from a high chair a thousand times and learns that it falls. Te takes that principle out of the kitchen and applies it everywhere: a portable rule, what she calls a "ghost in the mind". Its truth lies in rational usefulness, not in any final scientific certainty.

Beebe's keywords: regulating, *planning*, and at its most developed, enforcing, in the sense of principles everyone can follow.

## In everyday life

- When a group stalls, you start dividing the goal into steps and roles.
- You judge ideas mainly by whether they work in practice.
- Ticking off finished tasks is satisfying.
- You prefer evidence and numbers to back a decision.
- You notice inefficiency quickly and want to fix it.
- Even a relaxed day off goes better with a rough plan.

Myers adds that extraverted thinkers can be convinced by reasoning, and that once they are convinced and decide, it gets done.

## What it is not

**Not coldness.** Thomson reminds us that thinking types have died for their principles; they register emotional effects differently. Displays of feeling can look like play-acting to them.

**Not introverted thinking.** Te asks whether something works and whether we can agree on it. Ti asks whether it holds together internally, whatever others think. Jung noted each tends to see only the worst in the other.

**Not simply being bossy.** Bossiness can come from shadow Te. Beebe describes his own senex Te telling people "rather grossly what their priorities ought to be".

## When it leads

ESTJs and ENTJs lead with Te. Myers describes them using thinking "to run as much of the world as may be theirs to run", abhorring confusion and half-measures, living by a code they believe others should follow too. Her warning: the basic mistake is inflicting one's own judgment on other people.

For ISTJs and INTJs, Te is the helper that carries an inner store of experience or vision out into practical structure. Thomson's advice to INTJs: "After ecstasy, the laundry."

## When it is the weakest note

For ISFPs and INFPs, Te is the inferior. Thomson describes it surfacing as passive resistance, agreeing and then doing otherwise, or as suddenly sorting people into good and bad by a rigid system. Under strain it can come out as unusually harsh criticism or frantic over-organising. Freud, whom Beebe reads as Fi-led, developed his Te theory late, and handed out rings to loyal followers, a sign of dogma.

## In the shadow

Te in a critical-parent role becomes dogmatic: insisting on the tried and true, arguing against any change. As a trickster, it uses rules and procedure to trap: "I'd love to, but policy…"

## Growing it

If Te is quiet for you, pick one small goal and write the next three concrete steps with a date, then check them off. Myers' description of thinking used well is "analysing the probable consequences"; its misuse is criticising anyone who holds the opposite view.
