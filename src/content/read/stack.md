If everyone uses all eight functions, why do some come easily and others feel like walking with a stone in your shoe? The classic answer is that the functions develop in an order, and that the order shapes who we become.

## One captain

Jung believed that, for practical life, one function has to lead. Adaptation needs clear aims, and two functions pulling with equal force produce confusion rather than balance.

> This absolute sovereignty always belongs, empirically, to one function alone, and can belong only to one function.
> — Jung, *Psychological Types*, p. 514

Myers used a ship. It needs a captain with undisputed authority. This leading function is called the **dominant**. It is the one you trust without thinking, the one that feels most like you, and usually the one you developed earliest.

## A helper facing the other way

A captain alone is not enough. Jung saw that the dominant is always accompanied by a second, supporting function, different from it in kind: a perceiving function supports a judging one, or the reverse. Myers added that the helper usually takes the opposite attitude. If your dominant works inwardly, your **auxiliary** works outwardly, and so on.

> Adequate, but by no means equal, development of a second process, not as a rival … but as a welcome auxiliary.
> — Isabel Myers, *Gifts Differing*, p. 11

This is why the four-letter codes work the way they do. An INFJ, for example, leads with introverted intuition (Ni) and is helped by extraverted feeling (Fe). An ESTP leads with extraverted sensing (Se) and is helped by introverted thinking (Ti).

## The third and the fourth

Opposite the dominant sits the **inferior** function: the same kind of process (perceiving or judging) but its opposite. If you lead with intuition, your inferior is sensing; if you lead with thinking, it is feeling. It also takes the opposite attitude. An INFJ's inferior is extraverted sensing.

Jung was careful to say inferior means *psychologically* behind, not defective. It stays relatively undeveloped because the dominant was given all the practice. In daily life it is the function that happens *to* us rather than the one we direct:

> The superior function is always the expression of the conscious personality … whilst the inferior functions belong to the things that happen to one.
> — Jung, *Psychological Types*, p. 426

Between auxiliary and inferior sits a **tertiary** function, the opposite of the helper. The books disagree about its attitude, and Thomson describes it as surprisingly unreliable: under stress it tells us exactly what we want to hear.

## How it grows

The most practical idea in this whole subject comes from Thomson and Myers together. When you feel stuck, the temptation is to escape into the dominant ("do more of what I'm good at") or to wrestle directly with the inferior. Neither works. Growth usually comes from deliberately engaging the **helper**, which brings in the opposite attitude and loosens the grip of the dominant.

> This feeling of being stuck is always the give-away.
> — Lenore Thomson, *Personality Type*, p. 84

Jung said the same thing in his own language: the way to the inferior lies through the auxiliary, never straight at it. Myers adds that the third and fourth functions are best cultivated later in life, once the first two are secure. Pushed too early, the effort merely diverts.

## What a stack is not

A stack is not a ranking of skills. A strongly intuitive person can be good at detail; a feeling type can reason brilliantly. The order describes what you *prefer* and what *energises* you, which is why Octanoia's questions ask about what comes naturally and what you enjoy, not what you are good at.

Beebe also warns against reading the numbers as strict strength levels. The positions are better thought of as roles than as rungs on a ladder, which is the subject of the next chapter.
